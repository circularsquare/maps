# T-854: replacing the car-free split (design, 2026-10-21)

Built 2026-10-21 (late), demand agent: see "Build" at the end. The design and measurements below
are as they were when Anita decided.

Anita, 2026-10-21: remove the dependence on car ownership, which makes the US hard to play. Her pick
for the replacement: **parking at the home end too, at about half the destination's rate, from
residential density**. Her second idea: riders who differ in how much they like rail, preferably as
a heavier tail on the existing choice curve rather than a separate preference. This note measures
today's effect, the options, and proposes stages. No code changed.

## Reading

- **The car-free split is worth 5-17% of a real network's rail trips**, and most in Tokyo. With
  nobody car-free the real networks lose: New York 6.6%, Paris 5.1%, London 10%, Tokyo 16.5%.
  New York's commuter rail loses 23% (2.36M to 1.82M riders), its subway 7.5%: a suburban home
  without a car takes the train on any long trip, since "other" at 12 km/h is hopeless there.
- **It is not what makes US suburbs hard.** US suburbs are already 2-5% car-free, so removing the
  split moves them 1-3 points. At the ceiling, suburbs (homes under 3,000 people/km²) reach 79-82%
  rail of rail + car in every city. What the split does is lift *other* countries' suburbs, which
  take national or prefecture figures (Tokyo 18-27% car-free, London 5-8%, Paris 13%). With a
  typical metro service, suburbs get 29% rail in New York against 36-39% in London, Paris and
  Tokyo. Without the split they all come out at 27-33%. The fix makes things fairer by bringing
  the others down to the US level, not by lifting the US.
- **Dropping it alone fails test A**: New York 83.5%, London 82.8% (target 85).
- **Home-end parking at half the rate brings the ceilings back and keeps dense cores where they
  are.** A: New York 86.9%, London 86.6%, Paris 88.6%, Tokyo 89.0%. B: 80-84%. Dense cores, served
  by a typical metro, end a few points above their present rail share. **A factor of about 0.4
  lands them on it**: 0.25 is below and 0.5 above in every city measured. Suburbs: no change from
  dropping the split, since nobody pays at under 3,000 people/km².
- **Rail-leaning riders as a heavier tail** (5% of riders choosing on a curve four times
  flatter) puts a small floor under slow lines (+1-3 points in suburbs) and costs about 1 point
  of test A. That still passes alongside home parking at 0.5. Every tail form tested does about
  the same; the explicit classes cost the most on A. The effect is small: I'd leave it out for
  now (Questions).

## Method

- **Real networks in the game.** My own Vite (6177), headless Chrome with a temporary profile.
  Each run starts a new sandbox in the city, imports the real save (New York `T-007-real-nyc.save`;
  London, Paris and Tokyo `data/packs/real/<id>.save`), and waits until demand is final and stable
  for 20 seconds. The switch: Playwright intercepts each pack's `.bin` download (not Range
  requests) and rewrites its `nocar` array. The settings are `zero` (nobody car-free), `flat` (every
  cell at the pack's own mean), `dens` (one density curve, below) and `real`. No change to the main
  tree's code or packs. Figures are the city's own rail trips a day.
- **Ceiling tests and made-up services.** The same code as T-636's harness, in a private copy of
  the crate (test file added as a module; the shared crate untouched). Anita's A and B, plus:
  - **T**, a typical metro reached on foot: 30 km/h, 6 minutes' walk each end, a train every
    6 minutes, $1.50 + $0.10/km;
  - **S**, a slow, sparse line: 20 km/h, 10 minutes' walk each end, every 15 minutes, the same
    fare.

  Every commute zone pair and everyday sample uses the model's own drive, parking, walk and
  car-free expressions. New are only the home-end parking and the tails. Rail is given as rail of
  rail + car (other left out, as in the ceiling tests), by the home zone's people per km² of land:
  under 3k, 3-10k, 10-20k and over 20k. Run time: 1-3 minutes a pack.
- **Home-end parking as measured**: f x the destination's paid-space rule, applied to residential
  density instead of jobs. That is f x min(15 ln(d / 3,000), 50) perceived minutes on every car
  trip from a home zone with d people per km² of land, with nobody car-free. At f = 0.4 a home in
  a zone of 40,000/km² pays 16 minutes (about $5), 25,000 pays 13, 10,000 pays 7, 5,000 pays 3,
  and under 3,000 nothing.
- Scripts and outputs kept in `notes/T-854/`: `run.mjs`, `batch.mjs` and `nocarpack.mjs` (the
  game runs), `t854_ceiling.rs` (the harness, run from a copy of the crate), `ceil_<pack>.json` and
  `<city>_<mode>.json`. The Python helpers (`copy.py`, `ceil_all.py`, `fit.py`, `table.py`,
  `browser_table.py`) stayed in the session scratchpad and are gone.

## The options

**(a) Drop it; everyone has a car.** No data. Removes the split, the "other" mode and the per-cell
`nocar` plumbing. Rail falls 5-17% on real networks, dense cores most (T over 20k: New York 85
to 67), and test A fails (83%). Not enough alone.

**(b) Home-end parking from residential density (Anita's pick).** Uses the pack's `pop` and its
zones, which every pack has: no new data, one rule worldwide. Run-time cost: a constant per home
subzone added to the car's cost, as `sz.nocar` is read now, so nothing measurable. With (a)'s
removals it simplifies the model on net. At f 0.4-0.5 dense cores keep or slightly gain rail,
the ceilings pass, and suburbs everywhere drop to the US level. Risks: a pure function of density
cannot see income or country, so one dense rich area and one dense poor area behave alike (the
same as the destination rule already does). Countries where cities are dense but car ownership is
low for other reasons (Lagos, Mumbai) lose their car-free riders, though "other" stood for their
minibuses only crudely anyway.
- The factor: 0.4 lands dense cores on their present rail share for both T and S, in New York,
  London and Tokyo. 0.5 puts them 3-6 points above it (New York over 20k: T 89 against 85).
- Whether it applies to a ride to a station: yes in the design (the household still keeps a car
  at home). Not measured.
- The regional layer has no car-free group today; leave home parking out of it (long trips, where
  a few minutes hardly count).

**(c) Car ownership that responds to the network.** Homes near good rail give up cars over time.
It needs a feedback loop (ownership from accessibility, then demand from ownership), a time
constant, saves that carry it, and a rule nobody can check. It would be fun, but it is the
dynamic part that makes play hard to read, and it would bring back the gap between countries
unless it starts from the same base everywhere. Later, if at all; (b) gives the static half of it.

**(d) One national constant everywhere.** Measured as the pack mean everywhere: real networks
change by 1% or less, ceilings rise 1 point, and every suburb gains (New York T under 3k 29 to
40) while cores lose. It keeps the split, "other" and the national-figure plumbing, and it puts
car-free people in US suburbs who are not there. Worse than now on the US problem.

**(e) The car-free share from one density curve** (not asked; measured because it is cheap): the
split as now, but the share comes from the zone's density through the US-England curve
everywhere. Real networks -4% to +1%, ceilings about as now. It keeps "other" and still says who
can drive. A fallback if (b) disappoints.

## Rail-leaning riders (Anita's second idea)

All forms measured on top of home parking 0.5. NY / London. "S suburbs" is the slow line from
homes under 3k; "S all" is the slow line overall.

| Form | A | B | S suburbs | S all |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| none | 86.9 / 86.6 | 80.4 / 80.4 | 5 / 9 | 19.9 / 22.3 |
| tail: 5% at scale 0.025 | 85.9 / 85.7 | 79.6 / 79.5 | 6 / 10 | 20.8 / 23.3 |
| tail: 10% at 0.025 | 84.9 / 84.7 | 78.8 / 78.7 | 8 / 11 | 21.8 / 24.2 |
| classes: 5% count rail at half, 5% never rail | 83.6 / 83.6 | 77.6 / 77.7 | 7 / 11 | 21.4 / 23.8 |
| classes: 10% and 10% | 80.4 / 80.4 | 74.8 / 74.9 | 9 / 12 | 22.9 / 25.2 |
| floor: rail between 2% and 98% where offered | 85.8 / 85.6 | 79.5 / 79.6 | 7 / 11 | 21.7 / 24.1 |
| floor 5% | 84.1 / 84.1 | 78.2 / 78.3 | 11 / 14 | 24.4 / 26.8 |
| tail on the cost ratio: 10%, (car / rail)^2 | 86.8 / 86.6 | 79.9 / 80.0 | 6 / 10 | 20.9 / 23.3 |
| home 1.0 with tail 10% at 0.025 | 86.5 / 86.9 | 80.9 / 81.8 | 8 / 11 | 27.3 / 29.3 |

- **It overlaps with the soft threshold, as Anita suspected.** The current curve already gives a
  slow line something (S suburbs 5-9%). Every form adds 1-3 points there and takes 1-6 from A,
  because the same riders who board a bad train also drive past a good one. The car-only class
  is what caps A hardest. Tokyo, Paris and Houston behave the same (Houston's S: 6.3 with no
  tail, 9.1 with tail 10%, 12.8 with floor 5%).
- **Simplest and most natural: the tail inside the existing curve** (a blend of the present logit
  with a flatter one for a few percent of riders). To a player it reads the same as now: the
  cheaper option wins, a little less absolutely. It decays smoothly, so a train an hour worse gets
  under 1% and one two hours worse 0.2%. Run time: one more three-way choice per pair lane, a
  small part of a pass whose cost is mostly the station logsums (expect a few percent; to measure).
- The **floor** is easiest to say in words ("any train that serves a trip gets at least 2%"), but
  it is a step: a useless detour line takes 2% of every pair it touches, which a player could
  farm. The **classes** do the same as the tail at about three times the choice cost and cost
  more on A. The **ratio** form does almost nothing for slow lines.
- **Does it needlessly complicate?** At these sizes, mostly yes: the gain on a slow line is
  1-3 points. That is real but hard to see in play next to station choice and crowding. A
  heavier tail that is visible (10%+) pushes A under 85 unless home parking is raised to compensate
  (the last row). If it goes in, 5% at 0.025 is the size that keeps both tests.

### Stage 2: the curve's floor and ceiling

Form (`Params::rail_bounds`, `rail_floor` 0.05, `rail_floor_ratio` 3): where rail is offered, rail's
share R of the trips not walking becomes lo + (1 - lo - hi) R and walking keeps its share. lo is
5% while rail's perceived cost is at most the car's (the car's mean over free and paying parkers),
falling linearly to 0 at 3 times the car's; hi mirrors it on car / rail (5% while the car costs at
most rail's, 0 when rail costs a third of the car's). Applied after the free/paid mix in the
commute lanes (F8, same expressions as the scalar), the everyday pass and the trip explorer.

Why this form: a floor on rail of rail + car rather than of all trips leaves walking alone (a short
trip's walkers are not handed to a train); bounding it by the cost ratio is what stops the farming
the design warned of (a line three times worse than driving gets nothing, at twice it gets half the
floor, and the plain logit already gives it 4%); the mirrored ceiling is Anita's "some never take
it", and it fades where rail is far better, so test A loses only a little. A ratio rather than a
difference in minutes, so a long trip and a short one are judged alike. Sizes tried (harness,
variants `bA/R` = floor and ceiling A% fading to none at R times, `fA/R` = floor only):
