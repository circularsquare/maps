## Why the sheet did not condense

Measured on a copy of her save (1,604 complexes, 132 lines; box 4,515 by 2,603 km):

- Every place's planned scale is the larger of what its stations ask and the **floor** (0.6 of a 1 km hop's scale on a
  country map). Her city networks ask 4.3 to 6.7 pitches a km (stations 1.5-3 km apart); the floor is 3.3. So the
  plan for the whole continent was within a factor of 2 everywhere, and the sheet drew it nearly uniform: networks 3.8
  pitches a km, empty land 3.35, water 3.36. Networks held 25% of the drawing's width (24% on the ground).
- Lowering the floor far from the networks does not work by itself, and that is geometry, not tuning (it is what Anita
  said in round 2: "its not actualyl like mathematically possible to distort stuff within big uninterrupted areas of
  land to make cities look bigger"). To draw a city 10-20 times bigger than the land round it, the land in a ring round
  it must stretch 10-20 times more one way than the other. The sheet holds any cell from stretching over about 2.3:1
  (the fold guard) and charges land near lines most for it. Tried (all on her save): the floor falling with distance
  from the networks, past 25 km, as a power or at the doubling limit's rate; far land and water nearly free to stretch
  unevenly (0.02-0.3); the fold guard starting at 9:1 in far cells; size pulls on far land up to 0.5; a second "raise"
  round. Best of them: empty land planned at 0.15 pitches a km came out at 0.7, the networks shrank to 40-75% of what
  they ask, up to 161 cells folded, the solve did not settle (stations still moving 6 pitches a round at the end), and
  the pictures were blobs, with bubbles round each lonely stop.

## The design: cut empty bands

A map maker condenses a continent by cutting the empty bands out: the West's columns of desert and the Plains drawn
narrow, the corridors and cities kept. That is a distortion along each axis separately (a Cartesian fisheye, below),
which keeps horizontal and vertical lines straight and puts all its uneven stretch where a network's column crosses
another's empty row, in places nobody is looking at.

- **Network stations**: those with 2 others within 10 km. A lonely stop on a long line is not one (St George, Kansas
  City), so its column is cut like the land round it, and the long line crosses the cut band drawn short.
- **Columns and rows**: each column of the sheet's finest lattice whose middle is more than 10 km across from every
  network station gets a rest width under 1: past 10 km, 1 / width grows linearly with the distance, so the width
  halves every 40 drawn pitches at the floor's scale. That is the field's own limit (Anita's "a city can be very
  enlarged, but it cant sit right next to an area thats very shrunk"; the scale may double at most every 40 pitches),
  so a cut band starts as gently as the field may change. At least 0.02. Rows the same.
- **The sheet keeps the cut shape**: each cell's rest shape is its columns' width by its rows' height, instead of a
  square. All the sheet's costs (uneven stretch, size to the plan, the fold guard, the raise) are measured against that
  rest shape, so land near lines keeps the cut shape as it kept the real one. The lattice, the raster, the warp and
  everything after the sheet (lines, carry, water, borders) are unchanged: places are still real places; only where
  the sheet puts them changed.
- **Country maps only** (600 km across or more, the code's existing split). On New York's map the cut took Long
  Island's east end and the box's margins (columns 0.76 of their width on average), and Long Island's shape kept fell
  from 0.88 to 0.81; a city map's margins are its context.

## What it does not do

- The networks are still small spots in a lot of land: the cut removes empty columns and rows, but land in a network's
  column and another network's row (the Gulf of Mexico in Florida's rows, Ontario in Toronto's column) stays at the
  floor. Drawing the networks much bigger than everything round them in two dimensions needs the sheet to let far land
  and water stretch far more unevenly than its fold guard allows, and a solver that settles under it (proposed as a
  follow-up, not filed: no id).
- **Zone borders in a cut band** became tall thin slivers (the Plains' zones between Denver and Chicago); round 2
  below draws only zones a line passes through.
- The cut is straight across the whole map, so a lonely stop's column is cut even where it has no neighbours at all.

## Round 2: borders, water and the empty part of a zone (after Anita's look)

Anita, 2026-11-06, on na-whole-before-after.png (relayed by maps-93): "hm yeah. okay i guess we need to not draw the zone
borders of regions that we dont actually pass through, so that squishing them looks less silly we can also omit drawing
water thats not touching a region we have stations in. should just drop out to white at a smooth octilinear edge. im
also noticiing that a lot of space is spent on the northeast part of the los angeles zone. which is mostly empty. i
guess cuz we're trying to rpeserve the aspect ratio of that zone. but here that doesn't matter tha tmuch, and we should
be willing to squeeze it, cuz it only has like one or two stops in it."

What changed (`sheetLines.ts` `sheetMap`, option `allZones` off; `sheet.ts` `freeKm` 25):

1. **Borders only of zones a line passes through**: a zone with a station, or one a hop's track crosses (its track
   sampled at the raster's cell). A border between two other zones is not drawn. Any map.
2. **Water only near those zones** (a map 600 km across or more): every water cell takes the zone of the nearest land
   (a breadth-first spread from all land over the water); water whose nearest land is not such a zone is left as plain
   background. The edge between kept and dropped water is traced and fitted like any coast, so it is octilinear.
   Lakes inside other zones go; the sea off a coast with lines stays out to halfway to the next coast without.
3. **Land far from the networks is free to squeeze** (same maps): land farther than 25 km from every network station
   costs aWater (0.1) to stretch unevenly instead of 4 or 2, even along a lonely line's track or inside a zone with
   stations elsewhere. Effect on her save: networks get what they ask (the biggest 6.11 -> 6.93 pitches a km, the
   others likewise), width share 0.48 -> 0.51. All Japan unchanged within noise (Honshu / Hokkaido / Kyushu / Shikoku
   kept 0.91 / 1.03 / 1.02 / 1.05; at 12 km Shikoku moved to 1.01, so 25 km it is). New York untouched (a city map).

## Looked outward

- Sarkar and Brown, "Graphical fisheye views of graphs" (CHI 1992; tech report https://cs.brown.edu/research/pubs/techreports/reports/CS-93-40.html)
  and Bostock's explainer (https://bost.ocks.org/mike/fisheye): the Cartesian fisheye distorts x and y separately with one
  monotone function each, so lines parallel to the axes stay straight, which they note suits orthogonal drawings. The
  cut is a Cartesian distortion with many foci (every network's columns) and a fall-off set by the sheet's own limit.
- Furnas's degree of interest (a priori importance minus distance; via Jakobsen and Hornbaek's review): the reason a
  place far from every network gets less room. Here the importance is "a network station is in this column".
- Wang, Lee and Tai (2008) and Wang and Chi (2011), already the sheet's basis (notes/T-2566.md): their context cells
  shrink only as far as a uniform-scale cell can; this task met that limit directly (above).
- Cameron Booth's Amtrak map (https://www.maproomblog.com/2016/09/cameron-booths-amtrak-subway-map/): designed from the
  Northeast corridor outward, the West's long lines drawn as straight runs across a compressed interior; the same split
  as the cut, by hand.
