## Today's diagram (the clean slate; agent sheet, 2026-11-05)

The full rules are docs/ui-diagram.md. In one page:

- **The graph.** Nodes are station complexes at their real places (metres); edges are hops between consecutive
  complexes of a line, each with its track on the ground.
- **Targets.** Each hop gets a drawn length (its target) from its real length: `1000 m x (len / 1000 m)^(1 - 0.7 d)`, d the
  **distortion dial** (0.5 now), so long hops shrink and short ones grow. Lonely hops (single-line stretches between
  cities) and hops between plain stops get a further 0.4 (at d = 1). Big sparse regions get a floor; parallel hops share
  one scale; hops across water get at least twice their real length at the local scale. The **pitch** (a line's stroke
  spacing) is the median hop over 9; everything below is in pitches.
- **Blocks.** Cities (10+ stations) are laid out alone in A and then moved as rigid units in one least-squares stage, with
  gaps of 16 pitches between their boxes. Stations between cities move on their own.
- **A** (10 to 40 rounds of least squares): hops at their targets along their real bearings; near pairs kept on their
  sides; crossings on the ground held; straits and bays held open (sea pairs at 1.25x their real distance).
- **B** (30 rounds): each edge pulled to its nearest free 45-degree direction, unconnected stations pushed apart.
- **The snap, the routing, the separation, the spread.** Pieces between key stations are made exactly octilinear (one
  straight run, or two legs), then a projection solver meets only inequalities (spacing, order, gaps), then stations
  slide along pieces to clear close pairs. This is the stage that makes most of the scale jumps (T-2561: p90 of the
  neighbour scale ratio 1.9 after B, 2.6 drawn).
- **Water, after the layout.** The ground's sea and rivers come from the packs' 25 m masks (sea = water 2 km across; river
  = 120 m or more, followed only where a hop crosses). Rivers are bands routed on a pitch grid between the hops that
  cross them, as wide as the river at a "scale field" (the 16 nearest stations' local scales, smoothed). The sea is a
  grid over the drawing where each cell asks its 4 nearest drawn stations "carried back through your own local map,
  is this place sea on the ground?", then majority votes, strait rules, island rules, and an octilinear fit (`fitOcti`).
- **Borders.** Not the real zone outlines at all (T-2464 replaced the warped outlines Anita found wavy): each cell near the
  network takes the zone of the nearest pill, and the boundaries are fitted octilinear. So a prefecture's drawn shape
  is the shape of its stations' cloud, not of the prefecture.

The common thread: every place is carried into the drawing by **its nearest stations' own local maps**, and neighbouring
stations' maps disagree (New York: 1 hop in 5 with ends at over 2x different scales). Anything drawn between stations
(coasts, rivers, borders) inherits those jumps. Each fix above patched one symptom of that. A single continuous warp of
the real plane, which the stations and the water both follow, removes the cause.

## Looked outward (agent sheet; 8 searches)

- **Wang, Lee and Tai, "Focus+Context Visualization with Distortion Minimization"** (IEEE TVCG 14(6), 2008;
  https://cgvsl-ncku.github.io/Vis2008/template.pdf). The closest thing to Anita's idea, read in full: a uniform grid over
  the object; each cell's edges are asked to be its original edges times a **uniform scale s_k** (no rotation, no
  stretch); the focal cells get the user's magnification, every other cell's s_k is re-estimated each iteration from its
  current size (cube root of its volume ratio), so the context shrinks to fit; one anchor term (weight 0.001) and box
  bounds; solved by repeated linear least squares. Cells with nothing in them get weight 0, so empty space absorbs the
  distortion. Taken: the energy (per-cell uniform scale, least squares, iterate), and the idea that empty cells give way
  (Anita's "peninsulas don't have to be huge").
- **Wang and Chi, "Focus+Context Metro Maps"** (IEEE TVCG 17(12), 2011, doi 10.1109/TVCG.2011.205;
  https://ir.lib.nycu.edu.tw/handle/11536/14562). The same group on metro maps: octilinear directions and regular spacing
  as least-squares energies, with the focus route given more room. Their point that a plain fisheye warp breaks
  octilinearity is why the warp needs a station snap after it. (Full text not reachable; abstract only.)
- **Gastner and Newman 2004 (diffusion cartograms); Gastner, Seguy and More, "Fast flow-based algorithm for creating
  density-equalizing map projections"** (PNAS 115(10), 2018; https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07625). A density field is
  equalised by a flow; the map never folds, and runs in seconds on a 512 x 512 grid. Taken: the magnification has to be a
  smooth field first (they blur the density), and a grid of a few hundred cells a side is enough for a country. Not
  taken as the solver: a cartogram matches areas exactly with no control over how far anything moves, and Anita's cap on
  displacement and bound on scale change are easier as least-squares terms.
- **Bounded-distortion maps** (Lipman, "Bounded distortion mapping spaces for triangular meshes", SIGGRAPH 2012,
  https://www.wisdom.weizmann.ac.il/~ylipman/BoundedDistortion/bounded_distortion_may_1.pdf; Kovalsky et al.,
  "Large-scale bounded distortion mappings", 2015, https://shaharkov.github.io/LargeScaleBD.html). Hard bounds on each
  cell's stretch, met by alternating a global solve with a projection of each cell onto the allowed set. Taken: Anita's
  two limits fit this "solve, then clamp the cells that break the bound, then solve again" pattern; a full
  bounded-distortion solver is more than we need if the magnification field is already bounded before the solve.
- **Optimized scale-and-stretch** (Wang, Tai, Sorkine and Lee, SIGGRAPH Asia 2008,
  https://cgvsl-ncku.github.io/Image_Resizing): the same grid energy for image resizing, with important cells scaled
  uniformly and plain cells absorbing the stretch; their stated weakness is diagonal features. Confirms the energy and
  that a grid line bending term keeps it smooth.
- **Polygon schematization that keeps topology**: Buchin, Meulemans, van Renssen and Speckmann, "Area-preserving
  simplification and schematization of polygonal subdivisions" (ACM TSAS 2(1), 2016; doi 10.1145/2818373), and a survey
  (https://www.ruhr-uni-bochum.de/schematicmapping/papers/smw-listabarth-raposo.pdf). A staircase schematization, then
  edge moves that trade area across a boundary, never letting two edges meet. Taken: our `fitOcti` with a per-point
  tolerance below half the local gap is a cheap version of the same guarantee (no two shores can meet if each strays
  less than half the water's width); exact area keeping is not needed.
- **Bast, Brosi and Storandt, "Metro Maps on Flexible Base Grids"** (SSTD 2021,
  https://ad-publications.informatik.uni-freiburg.de/SSTD_Metro%20Maps%20on%20Flexible%20Base%20Grids.pdf): octilinear
  layouts on a grid whose density adapts to the input. A different route (exact layout on a grid graph, slow for 9,000
  stations), noted for later: a warped plane could serve as such a grid's geometry.

## Design

The order: magnification field -> sheet -> stations on the sheet -> octilinear lines -> water and borders through the
same sheet, octilinear last. One map from real to drawn places, used by everything.

1. **The grid.** A lattice of square cells over the stations' box plus a margin (8% of its diagonal, at least 8 km),
   256 nodes along the longer side, whatever the map's size: about 1.2 km cells for New York, 10 km for all Japan. Each
   cell is two triangles; inside a triangle the warp is linear, so a place, a coast point and a station all go through
   the same piecewise-linear map, and the map can be inverted cell by cell to draw rasters.
2. **What drives the magnification** (a scale per station, in pitches per real metre, then a field):
   - **room**: a station needs its pills and bundles to fit beside its neighbours: the median over its 4 nearest
     stations of (its strokes + theirs) / 2 + `gap` pitches, over their real distance. This is what makes cores big at
     any dial, as today.
   - **the dial**: today's hop targets, `1000 m x (len / 1000 m)^(1 - 0.7 d)` over the hop's real length, at today's
     pitch (the median target over 9). A station takes the median over its hops. So the dial still evens out hop lengths.
   - the station's scale is the larger of the two. The field at a lattice node is the geometric mean of the stations'
     scales weighted by a Gaussian of radius max(1.5 km, the station's 4th-nearest distance), blended with a **floor**
     (`0.25^d` of a 1 km hop's scale, T-2550's floor) where there are few stations. Land and sea with nothing on them
     sit at the floor: peninsulas and empty islands are not enlarged (Anita: "peninsulas dont have to be huge").
   - **demand** is left out on purpose for now: the room term already follows where lines and stations crowd, and a
     scale that changed with riders would move the map as the game runs. It is a one-line addition (raise a station's
     scale by its riders' share) if Anita wants busy places bigger than their stations alone ask for.
3. **Anita's two limits, as numbers.**
   - **Scale change per drawn distance**: between neighbouring places the scale may at most double over `D` = 40
     drawn pitches (about 280 px at the default zoom). Met by raising the low side (never shrinking the city): a sweep
     over the lattice in both directions, `log s_v >= log s_u - ln 2 x (drawn distance) / D`, until nothing changes.
     So Manhattan can be 4x Long Island's scale only with about 80 drawn pitches of gradual change between them.
   - **Displacement cap**: `2000 km x (extent / 4500 km)^0.62`, the extent the built box's diagonal. That gives about
     2,000 km for the contiguous US, 5,000 km for the whole world (Anita's figures), about 1,460 km for all Japan and 380
     km for New York. Displacement is measured against the best uniform scaling of the whole map (no turn), in real km.
     Where a lattice node is past the cap, it is pulled back to the cap (weight 1) and the sheet solved again, up to 4
     times. As Anita said, very lenient: in New York and Japan it never binds (below); it is a guard against runaway
     cases (one huge city at the edge of a big sparse country), not a shaping term.
4. **The energy and the solver.** Per lattice edge, its drawn vector should be its real vector times the field's
   scale there, with no turn (Wang, Lee and Tai's uniform-scale cells): a horizontal edge `dx = s h, dy = 0`, a vertical
   edge `dx = 0, dy = s h`, weight 1 each. Linear, and x and y separate: two sparse systems of one unknown per node
   (65k), solved by least squares. Cells may become rectangles and grid lines may bow, which is what Anita described;
   no cell may fold (checked: none did). After a solve, cells that came out smaller than the field asks (cores hemmed in
   by their neighbours) get their wish raised by 80% of the shortfall, bounded again and solved again, twice.
   - Cost: the prototype's plain conjugate gradients take about 1,000 to 1,300 iterations a solve, 6 solves, about 25 s
     in Node for either map. That is the solver's fault, not the problem's: these are 2D Laplace-like systems on a
     regular grid, which a multigrid solver (or solving a 64-lattice first and refining) does in well under a second.
     In the worker the sheet would cost less than today's A stage.
5. **Stations on the sheet.** Each station starts at its warped place. They should then move only a little to reach 45
   and 90 degree lines. Two ways were tried (prototype below):
   - a simple snap (each hop pulled to its nearest 45-degree direction, its length the sheet's, each station anchored
     to its warped place, the anchor stiffened past 4 pitches): stations stray 2.5 pitches (median), and 53% (New York) to 65% (Japan) of hops end
     up exactly octilinear; the rest are drawn as two legs. Scale jumps stay low (p90 1.47 against today's 2.56).
   - today's octilinear stages run as a rebuild from the warped places (B, the snap, the routing, the separation):
     90% octilinear, but stations stray 7 pitches (median) and up to 48, and the jumps come back (p90 2.19), because B
     and the separation pull every hop toward today's targets and spacing, not the sheet's.
   - **The design**: keep today's pieces, routing and separation (they are what make the drawing clean: pills, bundles,
     one bend per piece), but feed them the sheet: (a) start from the warped places, no A and no blocks stage (the sheet
     replaces both); (b) every hop's target is its length on the sheet, not the dial's; (c) a term in B, the snap and the
     separation holding each station within about 4 pitches of its warped place (soft, weight rising past it), like
     today's rebuild pull but to the sheet; (d) the separation's spacing pushes act along pieces only. Today's crossings,
     sides and parallel terms become unnecessary in the main: the sheet keeps real relative places, so lines that cross
     on the ground cross, parallels stay side by side, and stations keep their sides, up to the stray.
6. **Water and borders through the same sheet, then octilinear.**
   - The real raster: the packs' 25 m water masks (all water, not only the sea) and the zones' outlines
     (`outlines.json`), sampled at 50 m for a city map, coarser for a country (450 m for all Japan: the sea and the straits that part land masses, no rivers), as
     labels: water, or the zone the land is in.
   - Carried into the drawing cell by cell through the warp (each drawn grid cell's middle mapped back to the real
     raster through its triangle), on a grid of 1 pitch (coarser for a big drawing, at most 4 million cells).
   - Then: each pill and 1.5 pitches round it is land; one 3x3 majority pass; water under 40 square pitches and land
     without a station under 30 dropped.
   - Coasts traced as rings and fitted with `fitOcti` (runs at 0, 45, 90 degrees) with a tolerance per point of at
     most 0.35 of the water's width across from it and 0.35 of the land's width behind it (found by climbing each
     side's distance field to its ridge), at most 10 pitches, and 1.5 pitches short of the nearest pill. Two shores of a
     strait each stray at most 0.35 of its width, so at least 0.3 of it stays open: **no strait closes and no two islands
     merge by the fit**, by construction. Corners rounded at 3 pitches.
   - Zone borders: the edges between two zones' land, chained from coast or junction to the next, fitted the same way
     (at most 6 pitches, 1 pitch short of a pill). These are the **real** borders, warped (Anita: "would solve the issue
     of region borders not having their right shape"); a station is on its zone's side wherever its stray is less than
     its distance to the border.
   - Rivers too narrow to show as area (the Harlem River at 2 pitches, small rivers away from cores) vanish in the
     cleaning. Where a line crosses such a river, today's river bands (T-2169) can still draw it at the least width,
     now routed along the warped centre line, which is where the stations around it really are.
7. **The dial** keeps its meaning: it sets the dial term in each station's scale and the floor, so 0 draws hops near
   their real lengths (only room enlarges cores) and 1 evens hop lengths fully. The limit `D` is a second, independent
   knob: how fast the scale may change. It could be tied to the dial (a lower dial, a gentler `D`) if one knob is wanted.
8. **Edits (stability).** The field is a sum of local Gaussians, so a station added or removed changes it only within
   a few of its neighbours' distances, and the bound spreads that change at most as far as the scale differs. The sheet
   is re-solved from the last solution (a few multigrid cycles); far away it moves by a near-uniform shift, which the
   camera can take out by pinning the drawing's middle. Stations keep their order and sides. Water and borders are
   redrawn from the new sheet each time (they are cheap). Worth measuring before building: today's rebuild moves 0.05 to
   0.15 hops (T-2556); the target would be the same or less.
9. **Lattice resolution** (found in the prototype). A fixed 256 lattice is 10 km cells for all Japan, and the steepest
   change a lattice can draw is one cell. Without a limit, Tokyo's scale fell tenfold within one cell and the sheet folded
   almost everywhere; with a limit of 1.6x per cell the sheet is clean but Tokyo's core gets too little room (98 station
   pairs closer than 1.5 pitches, against 9 in New York). The fix is finer cells where the field is high: a quadtree
   lattice (cells split where the scale is above the floor), or a uniform 2-3 km lattice solved by multigrid. New York's
   1.2 km cells are fine (the per-cell limit never bound there; 128 nodes gave the same figures as 256).

## Open questions for Anita

1. **How big should cores be against the countryside?** The sheet, as tuned, draws Manhattan about half as big as today
   and Long Island and Hokkaido bigger, so the whole map reads more like the real one and dense cores are tighter. One
   knob turns it either way. I'd go about halfway back toward today's cores.
2. **How fast may the size change?** Now: at most double every 40 pitches (about 4 cm on screen at the default zoom).
   Gentler looks more like the real map but squeezes the cores; steeper gives room but lets a city sit next to shrunk
   land. 40 looks right to me in the pictures.
3. **Busy places bigger by demand?** I left it out: the map would change size as ridership changes. Say if you want it.
4. **Narrow rivers** (the Harlem River, small rivers in the suburbs) are too thin to show as water at their real width.
   Keep today's rule: draw one as a thin band only where a line crosses it?
5. **Real borders** (states, prefectures) come back with the sheet, in their real shape. A station near a border may
   then sit a few pitches on the wrong side after the lines are straightened. Fine, or should the border bend round it?

## Recommendation

Build it, changed in two ways, in stages: the sheet is clearly better for what Anita asked (smooth scale, the Sound, the
core rivers, real coasts and borders, islands that read), and it removes most of the water and border special cases. But
not as prototyped: (1) the octilinear stages must be today's pieces and separation held to the sheet, not a simple snap
nor today's stages as they are (T-2567); (2) the solver must be multigrid on a lattice fine in the cores, so all Japan's
cores get their room and the sheet costs under a second (T-2568). Water and borders from the sheet go in after T-2567,
since they need the stations to stay on the sheet.
