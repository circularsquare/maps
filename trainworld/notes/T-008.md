# T-008 track model and capacity: design

Done 2026-10-08. Design only, no code. The settled result is in SPEC section 6; this note keeps
the reasoning, the numbers and the options that were turned down. Numbers were checked with a
throwaway script (curve speeds, capacities, the delay table, save size); sources at the end.

Requirements carried in (SPEC 1, 2, 2.1, 6, 11; DECISIONS 2026-10-08): one track type; curvature
sets the speed limit; levels -3..+3 with ramps; build anywhere, water ~5x; no buildings, yards or
depots; no signals, but the track is a directed segment graph and trains reach the renderer as
keyframes so signals stay possible; tangles cost run time through segment and junction capacity
with the delay baked into run-time profiles; money present but not tight; saves are inputs only;
edits are cheap and dirty only what they touch; never freeze.

---

## 1. Geometry

### 1.1 Arcs and straights, no transition curves, no splines

Track is a chain of **straight pieces and circular arcs**, tangent-continuous (no kinks), with
curvature allowed to jump where an arc meets a straight.

Why not the alternatives:

- **Clothoid transitions** (what real railways build) only change where the curvature ramps up.
  The run-time profile already ramps speed between limits at the train's acceleration and braking
  rates, so the speed effect is a second or two per curve. At map zoom the difference is invisible
  (the arc shifts inward by `L²/24R`: 1-3 m for normal curves). Costs: Fresnel integrals to place
  and measure them, no closed-form offset curve, harder radius enforcement. A later cosmetic pass
  could ease the drawn line without touching the model.
- **Splines** (Bézier, Catmull-Rom) are easy to draw but curvature changes continuously, so the
  speed limit varies along every curve, the minimum radius is hard to guarantee, length needs
  numeric integration, and a parallel offset of a spline is not a spline. They also feel floaty.

What arcs and straights give: exact length in closed form, position at an offset in a few flops
(the clock worker's 50 m resampling and keyframes, T-002), one speed limit per arc, and a parallel
track at ±2 m is again arcs and straights, so double track is exact.

### 1.2 How the player draws: points of intersection with a radius

The model is how real alignments are designed: a polyline of **points of intersection (PIs)**,
with a circular arc fitted at each interior PI.

- Click to place PIs. Between PIs the track is straight; at each PI the tool fits the arc that
  turns from one leg to the next.
- **Auto radius** (the default, stored as 0): the largest radius whose tangent length
  `T = R tan(Δ/2)` fits in half of each adjacent leg (all of a leg whose other end is a track
  end), capped at the radius that already allows the train's top speed (1,800 m at 160 km/h).
  Wider is never slower, so auto is usually what the player wants.
- **Manual radius**: scroll over a PI (or drag its radius handle) to tighten or widen it; stored
  in whole metres. Under the minimum radius, or not fitting the legs, the arc shows red and the
  edit cannot be committed.
- While drawing, the preview runs from the last PI to the cursor; the arc at the last PI forms as
  soon as the next point is placed. The preview is coloured by speed limit, each PI shows radius
  and km/h, and the running cost shows by the cursor. Drag a placed PI to move it; the arcs at it
  and its two neighbours refit.
- **Snapping**: to a track end (continue it), onto existing track (makes a junction, 4.3), to a
  station. A track end attached to a node leaves along the node's heading: the first arc is the
  one tangent to that heading, so branches always leave smoothly, like a turnout.
- **Levels while drawing**: keys (or two dock buttons) set the level of the next PI; section 2.
- The preview for the one alignment being drawn is computed on the main thread (microseconds, the
  same geometry code compiled to a small WASM module or a TS port). The committed geometry is
  computed by the clock worker, the owner of the network, from the same quantised inputs.

Each arc is computed in a local metre frame at its PI (east-north at that latitude); straights
join tangent points. Nothing is solved on a plane larger than one curve, so long intercity
straights do not pick up projection error. Rendering converts to the city's local mercator units
(T-002).

### 1.3 Curve speed

`v = sqrt(a_lat * R)` with **a_lat = 1.1 m/s²** (total lateral acceleration, cant included).

Grounding: French LGVs are designed with 7,000 m curves for 320 km/h (160 mm cant + 100 mm cant
deficiency): 88.9² / 7,000 = 1.13 m/s². New York's 140 m (458 ft) curve from 42 St into Broadway
is held to 25 mph: 11.2² / 140 = 0.89 m/s². EN 13803 allows up to 160 mm cant plus 130 mm
deficiency for conventional trains (~1.9 m/s²), so 1.1 is a design value, not a limit, which suits
a game that wants curves to matter.

| Radius | Speed limit | | Speed | Radius needed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 m | 38 km/h | | 80 km/h | 450 m |
| 200 m | 53 km/h | | 120 km/h | 1,010 m |
| 300 m | 65 km/h | | 160 km/h | 1,800 m |
| 500 m | 84 km/h | | 200 km/h | 2,810 m |
| 1,000 m | 119 km/h | | 250 km/h | 4,380 m |
| 3,000 m | 207 km/h | | 320 km/h | 7,180 m |

- **Minimum radius 100 m** (38 km/h). Metro design standards say 250-300 m on main lines, but New
  York has 140 m curves on express track and a 45 m loop at City Hall; 100 m lets a player turn a
  Manhattan corner and pay for it in speed.
- **Speed cap**: the track has none of its own; the train's top speed caps it. For the one M1
  train kind that is **160 km/h** (common top speed of commuter and regional EMUs). A later faster
  kind (SPEC 6.3) runs on the same track and finds curves under ~7 km slow it, as on real HSR.
- Grades do not change speed in M1 (EMUs climb 4% fine at reduced acceleration). Turnouts have no
  separate speed limit: the branch's first arc sets it.

## 2. Levels and grades

| Level | Height of rail | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| +3 | +24 m | high viaduct, over two others |
| +2 | +16 m | viaduct over a viaduct |
| +1 | +8 m | viaduct (clears streets and at-grade track) |
| 0 | ground | at grade; roads ignored |
| -1 | -8 m | shallow tunnel, cut and cover |
| -2 | -16 m | bored tunnel, under a -1 |
| -3 | -24 m | deep bored tunnel |

- **8 m per level**: rail-to-rail clearance for one track over another with overhead wires and a
  structure (~5.5 m wire height + ~2 m deck). Uniform spacing keeps ramps uniform; real deep
  tunnels go deeper, but depth only matters to cost here.
- **Max grade 4%**: the usual limiting gradient for metro main lines (Washington's tunnels 4%,
  Boston and Philadelphia up to 5%; design guides prefer under 3.5%) and the steepest HSR (Köln-
  Rhein/Main). So a **ramp is 200 m per level**; 0 to -2 is 400 m, -2 to +1 is 600 m.
- **Where ramps go**: level is set per PI. Where two consecutive PIs differ, the ramp is centred on
  the leg between them at 4%; the track stays level on both sides. The leg must be long enough or
  the leg shows red. Centred means the result does not depend on which way the track was drawn.
- Nodes (junctions, crossings, stations) sit at whole levels, not on ramps, and a station's
  platform extent must be level.
- **Crossing**: two tracks cross freely where their heights at the crossing point differ by at
  least one level (8 m), ramps included. Same whole level: a **flat crossing** (4.3). In between:
  invalid, shown red.
- Ground is flat everywhere: no terrain model in M1. The level's look (tunnels faded, viaducts with
  a shadow) is T-023's.

## 3. Topology: what the player builds (open)

Three options. The graph in section 4 is written for C; B would change junction moves and line
paths (noted where it matters).

**A. Double-track route as one object, always** (Subway Builder). Draw once, get two tracks, one
per direction.

**B. Individual tracks** (NIMBY Rails). Each track drawn separately; lines choose tracks;
crossovers, loops, four-track and flyovers built by hand.

**C. Route object with a track count**: double by default, single track as an option (cheaper,
both directions share it, stations are passing points). Four-track (local and express) could be a
later count.

| | A: always double | B: individual tracks | C: double, single as option |
|---|---|---|---|
| Play feel | map-level planning; fast | model-railway depth; designing flyovers and throats is the fun for NIMBY players | as A, plus cheap rural branches and the single-track trade-off |
| Drawing | one stroke per route | two strokes per route, needs a parallel-track helper; crossovers by hand | one stroke; a toggle for single |
| Junctions | implied layout; flat or flying as a toggle | whatever was drawn | as A |
| Capacity model | streams per direction known; conflicts from a fixed junction rule | conflicts from geometry: diamonds, shared switches, opposing moves on one track over any length; correct but hard to explain to the player | as A, plus a simple single-track rule |
| Tangles | cannot draw a confusing throat | can, and NIMBY saves show players do | as A |
| Signals later | blocks per directed track; junction internals generated from templates | direct (NIMBY shows it works) | as A; single-track sections are blocks between passing points |
| Save size (10k km) | ~170 KB | ~2x PIs, ~300 KB | ~170 KB |
| Edit cost | small | small, but more objects per edit | small |

**Recommendation: C.** Anita's priorities are never freezing, low CPU, and tangles that cost in a
way the player can see and understand. With route objects every junction has a known internal
layout, so the delay at it can be explained in one sentence ("trains to the branch cross the
inbound main line"). B's freedom mostly adds ways to build tangles, which the capacity model then
has to explain from geometry. C adds single track over A because cheap single-track branches are a
real choice (intercity links to towns in M2) and cost almost nothing to model. **Open** for Anita:
it is a play-feel call, and B is a legitimate game (NIMBY's).

Under C:

- An edge has `tracks` = 2 or 1. The two tracks of a double edge are 4 m apart (drawn as two
  lines only when zoomed in). Running side (right in the US, left in Japan and the UK) comes from
  the city pack and decides which track each direction uses.
- **Flying junction** is a toggle on a junction: it removes the crossing conflict (4.3) and costs
  a flyover (6). The flyover is drawn as a cosmetic overpass, not modelled track.
- Two routes side by side (four tracks) are allowed; their stations at the same place are two
  stations joined by a walking transfer.

## 4. The stored graph

### 4.1 Inputs (what the save holds)

```
Node      id, position (lon, lat in 1e-6 degree), level, kind
          kind: end | junction (flying: bool) | crossing | station
Edge      id, node a, node b, tracks (1 | 2),
          PIs: [position, radius (0 = auto, else metres), level] between the end nodes
Station   node id, name, platform length (m, steps of 20), (later: extra platform tracks)
Line      id, name, colour, cars, dwell, trains per period [5],
          stops: [station node], hops: per stop-to-stop hop, [edge id + direction bit]
```

- An **edge** is one alignment between two nodes: no node in its interior. Drawing onto an
  existing edge splits it in two at the new node; the geometry is unchanged because an end
  attached to a node is tangent to the node's heading, and the arc tangent to the incoming leg and
  reaching that point with that heading is the original arc.
- A **node's heading** is the track tangent at it; every edge end at a node leaves along the
  heading or its reverse (that is what makes it a switch rather than a corner). Ports on the
  heading side are side B, the others side A.
- **Ends**: a dead-end node with one edge. A station at an end node is a terminus.
- Ids are stable u32s in memory (free lists, so undo and keyframes keep working); the save
  renumbers them densely.
- Positions are quantised to 1e-6 degree (~10 cm) when the player places them, so derived geometry
  is a deterministic f64 function of the save (no fast-math, as SPEC 7).

### 4.2 Derived (never saved)

- **Pieces**: per edge, the arcs and straights with start offset, length, speed limit, level and
  height at each end, water or land per piece (from the water mask, section 6).
- **Directed tracks**: per edge, one per direction for a double edge, one shared for a single edge.
  `(edge, direction)` is the "segment" in SPEC 2.1's keyframes `(segment, offset, speed, time)`.
- **Moves** at each node: every (port on side A, port on side B) pair, both ways. Under B, moves
  would come from the drawn switches instead.
- **Crossings**: found from a spatial grid (1 km cells) of piece bounding boxes; tested exactly;
  each becomes a flat crossing node, a clear grade separation, or an error.
- **Resources** for capacity (section 5) and **render buffers** per tile.

### 4.3 Junctions and crossings

- **Junction**: a node with three or more ports. Its moves conflict where their paths cross at
  the same level. With ports on each side ordered left to right and each double-track port's two
  tracks placed by running side, two moves cross when their track positions are in opposite order
  on the two sides: `(pA1 - pA2) * (pB1 - pB2) < 0`. Sharing a track at one end (a merge or a
  diverge) is not a conflict here: the trains are counted by the track capacity before or after.
  A plain double-track Y has exactly one crossing pair: trains to the branch on the far side cross
  the inbound trains from the other leg. A flying junction has none.
- **Flat crossing**: two routes crossing at grade with no connection. Every move on one route
  conflicts with every move on the other.
- **Grade-separated crossing**: two edges whose heights differ by one level or more at the crossing
  point. Not a node; no interaction.
- **Reversal at a through station** (a short-turn line): the reversing move crosses the opposite
  direction's track, so it conflicts with that direction's trains there.

### 4.4 Stations and platforms

- A station is a node on a route, with a **platform length** from 60 to 400 m in 20 m steps. The
  platform extends half its length each way along the adjacent edges, which must be level there
  and hold no other node or ramp. Curved platforms are allowed (New York has them).
- **Platform length caps train length**: a line's trains are at most `floor(shortest platform on
  its stops / 20 m)` cars (20 m cars; T-025/T-028 own car capacity).
- Platform tracks = the route's track count: two on double track, one on single track (where the
  station is also a passing loop, cost included). More platform tracks at a terminus are a later
  option.

### 4.5 How lines reference track

A line stores its **stops and the exact path between them** as edge ids with a direction bit. The
path is found by the tool (fastest path in the directed graph) when the line is drawn or rerouted,
and then stored, so:

- New track never silently reroutes an existing line; the player reroutes deliberately.
- A split edge rewrites the paths through it to its two halves (part of the split edit).
- Deleting an edge a line uses tries the fastest replacement path; with none, the line is marked
  broken and shown red until fixed.
- A line may pass a station without stopping (express).

### 4.6 Save size

Encoding: per section a count, then varints; positions as zigzag deltas from the previous point
of the same edge; radius 0 for auto; level as one byte per PI (compresses away); names as
UTF-8; then deflate (the browser's `CompressionStream`). Scratch estimate on a synthetic 10,000
route-km network (40% urban with a PI every 300 m and an edge every 1.2 km, 60% intercity with a
PI every 1.5 km and an edge every 8 km; 3,500 stations; 400 lines of 25 stops):

| Part | Raw |
|---|---|
| Nodes (3.7k) | 18 KB |
| Edges with 13k PIs | 110 KB |
| Stations with names | 61 KB |
| Lines with paths | 62 KB |
| **Total** | **251 KB raw, ~170 KB compressed** |

Dense hand drawing (a PI every 100 m everywhere, 96k PIs): 740 KB raw, ~575 KB compressed.
Storing derived geometry instead (a vertex every 50 m) would be 1.5 MB before compression. So a
huge save stays well under SPEC 11's "a few MB", and derive-on-load is a few ms to a few tens of
ms in the worker.

## 5. Capacity and delay

### 5.1 Resources

Everything trains share is a **resource** with an occupancy time `s` per train. Per period, each
resource's load is the scheduled trains per hour of every line using it (the player sets trains
per period, SPEC 6.3), and its utilisation is

    rho = sum over lines (trains per hour x s) / 3600

| Resource | Who uses it | Occupancy s per train |
|---|---|---|
| **Track section**: a run of directed track over which the set of lines does not change | trains in that direction | `90 s + v / 0.6 m/s²` (v = top speed reached on it) |
| **Single-track section**: between passing points (stations, junctions, double track) | trains both ways | run time across it + 60 s |
| **Platform track** at a stop | stopping trains; passing trains | dwell + 60 s; 40 s |
| **Terminus**: reversing trains, over its platform tracks | reversing trains | (layover + 60 s) / platform tracks |
| **Junction move**: a move plus the moves it crosses | trains on those moves | 90 s |
| **Flat crossing**: both routes, all moves | every train through | 90 s |

Where the numbers come from:

- **Track sections**: 90 s is a typical dwell and the margins of modern fixed-block signalling;
  `v / 0.6` grows the block length with braking distance (0.6 m/s² is a common signalling braking
  rate). That gives 33 trains per hour per direction at 40 km/h, 28 at 80, 25 at 120, 22 at 160,
  15 at 320. Real lines: London's Victoria line runs 36 with automatic operation, New York
  trunks 24-30, Tokyo's Chuo Rapid 30, commuter main lines 20-24, the Tokaido Shinkansen up to 17.
  Merging consecutive edges with the same lines into one section means a train meets each
  bottleneck once, not once per edge.
- **Single track**: a 5 km section at 80 km/h is ~319 s, so 11 trains per hour in total, both
  directions; 10 km at 120 km/h is 9. That is what makes single track cheap but limited.
- **Terminus**: two platform tracks with a 3 minute minimum turnaround is 30 trains per hour, close
  to what two-track terminals like New York's achieve (24-30).
- **Junctions and flat crossings**: 90 s covers clearing a 200 m train through the switches plus
  route setting. Two crossing moves can then share 40 trains per hour at most.

### 5.2 The delay curve

Delay per train at a resource, in seconds, from utilisation `rho`:

    mix = 1 - sum over streams (share of trains)²      streams = (line, direction)
    c   = 0.2 x mix
    W(rho) = c x s x rho / (1 - rho)                    for rho <= 0.9
           = W(0.9) + W'(0.9) x (rho - 0.9)             above 0.9 (straight line, stays finite)
           + 1800 s x (1 - 1/rho)                       added when rho > 1

- The first part is Kingman's queueing formula, `W = s rho/(1-rho) x (ca² + cs²)/2`, with the
  variability term small because trains are timetabled: `(ca² + cs²)/2` = 0.2 when every train is
  a different line, less when one line dominates. **One line alone gets no queueing delay**: its
  own trains are evenly spaced. Two lines at equal frequency get half (mix 0.5): their trains
  arrive at uncoordinated offsets.
- Above 0.9 the curve continues as a straight line with the slope it had at 0.9, so it is steep
  but never infinite.
- Over capacity (`rho > 1`), more trains are scheduled than fit. The fluid-queue term is the
  average extra wait if the queue builds for an hour and dispatchers then clear it: half an hour
  times the excess share. It applies to a single line too: running 36 trains per hour on a
  30-per-hour track is impossible, and this is how it shows.
- Constants: `c0 = 0.2`, `rho_k = 0.9`, overload window 3,600 s. All three are tuning knobs.

Delay per train in seconds for a 90 s junction (two equal lines, mix 0.5; many lines, mix 0.8)
and an 80 km/h track section (two equal lines):

| rho | Junction, 2 lines | Junction, many | Section, 2 lines |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 9 | 14 | 13 |
| 0.7 | 21 | 34 | 30 |
| 0.75 | 27 | 43 | 38 |
| 0.8 | 36 | 58 | 51 |
| 0.85 | 51 | 82 | 72 |
| 0.9 | 81 | 130 | 114 |
| 1.0 | 171 | 274 | 241 |
| 1.1 | 425 | 581 | 532 |
| 1.2 | 651 | 862 | 795 |
| 1.5 | 1,221 | 1,594 | 1,476 |

So a junction loaded like UIC 406's recommended peak (75-85%) costs half a minute to a minute per
train; a flat crossing of two routes at 15 trains per hour each way (each move sees its own 15
plus the other route's 30, rho = 1.125, three streams) costs about 10 minutes per train and turns
red. That is the intended steepness.

### 5.3 No iteration needed

The player fixes trains per hour; delay lengthens the round trip, which raises the number of trains
needed (the fleet, which costs money), but not the trains per hour through any resource. So loads
are a direct function of the schedule and every resource's delay is computed once, in one pass, in
the clock worker: ~10-20k resources and a few hundred lines is well under a millisecond. It does
not wait for the demand solve; demand uses its output (SPEC 2.1 is updated to say so).

### 5.4 Baked into run-time profiles

Per period and line direction, the profile (T-025) gets each resource's delay as a **hold at the
resource's entry**: the approach to the junction or section, or the platform for platform and
terminus resources. Delays are rounded to whole seconds and under 2 s are dropped.

- Under 30 s: a slower approach over the last few hundred metres that loses that time (trains
  crawl in).
- 30 s and over: the train brakes to a stop at the approach point, stands, and accelerates; the
  standing time is the delay minus the braking and acceleration loss.

Every train of the line gets the same average hold, so trains stay analytic. Per SPEC 2.1 a later
block simulator would replace these holds with real waits, producing the same keyframe shape.

### 5.5 How it shows

- **On the map**: resources at 75-90% are marked busy, 90-100% near full, over 100% overloaded
  (three marker colours from the token set, only on resources at 75% and up, so a healthy network
  shows nothing). The marker carries the delay per train, e.g. `+1:20`, for the period shown.
- **Trains** visibly crawl into or stand in front of an overloaded junction, every one of them.
- **Junction inspector**: the moves as a small diagram, trains per hour on each, which moves cross,
  utilisation bar, delay per train, and the hint to make it flying.
- **Line panel**: round trip split into running, dwell and delay, with the worst spots named
  ("delay 3:40, mostly at Queensboro junction"), and the trains needed, which the delay pushed up.
- Riders see longer trips through demand, so the mode-split bar drops too.

## 6. Costs

One price list everywhere in the world (Subway Builder's "Mediterranean" idea), in millions of
US dollars per route-km of double track, stations included separately.

| Level | Land | Over or under water |
|---|---|---|
| +3 | 100 | 160 |
| +2 | 80 | 130 |
| +1 | 60 | 100 |
| 0 | 20 | 100 |
| -1 | 90 | 180 |
| -2 | 130 | 210 |
| -3 | 170 | 220 |

- Single track: 0.6x. Ramps: priced at the dearer of their two levels.
- **Water**: at grade, 5x land (a low bridge: 100). The spec's "~5x" is read as five times at-grade
  land, not five times every level, because 5x a viaduct (300) would cost more than a deep tunnel
  and push every river crossing underground, while real cities bridge rivers more cheaply than they
  tunnel under them. Over water, viaducts are ~1.6x; tunnels under water 2x shallow (immersed tube),
  1.3x deep. Which pieces are over water comes from a water mask shipped in the city pack (and
  the world pack for intercity track), sampled per piece when geometry is derived; nothing in the
  pipeline makes one yet (follow-up task). Only build cost depends on it, and that is charged once
  and saved as money, so a later mask version does not change an existing save.
- **Stations**, 200 m platform, two platform tracks: level 0: 10, +1: 30, +2: 40, +3: 50,
  -1: 60, -2: 100, -3: 140. Other lengths: `cost_200 x (0.4 + 0.6 x length / 200)` (100 m: 0.7x,
  400 m: 1.6x). Single-track station with its loop 0.7x; over water 2x.
- **Junction** (switches): 0.25 km of track at its level (5 at grade, 32 at -2). **Flying
  junction**: plus 0.6 km of track one level away from the junction (36 at grade, 102 at -2).
  **Flat crossing**: 0.1 km of track at its level (2 at grade): cheap to build, dear in delay.
- **Removing** track refunds half its build cost (T-028 may tune this).

Grounding (Transit Costs Project): outside the US, mostly-tunnelled urban rail averages about
$215M/km and mostly at- or above-ground $52M/km, stations included; the cheapest full tunnels are
Catania ($61M/km) and Lyon ($106M/km); Spain and Turkey are consistently low. Here a bored line
with a station every km costs 130 + 100 = 230/km, an elevated line with a station every 1.5 km
60 + 20 = 80/km, at grade 20 + 7 = 27/km: in the same range. Relative to cut and cover, Subway
Builder uses deep bore 4.5x, bored 2.0x, elevated 0.8x, at grade 0.3x; this list is flatter for
deep (1.9x) because our -3 is only 24 m down.

**Starting money: $6B.** That buys one real first line, not a network: a 20 km bored line with 18
stations is 4.4B plus maybe 0.3B of trains, or about 70 km of elevated line with 50 stations. After
that, growth comes from fares (T-028 sets income and the clock rate, which decide how fast).

## 7. Edits and what they dirty

The clock worker owns the network. The main thread sends **edit operations** (add or move a PI,
change a radius or level, split, delete, add a station, change a line or its schedule); the worker
validates, applies, and answers with the cost, warnings and changed buffers. Every op has an
inverse, which is undo. Each op knows the edges and nodes it touched; from those:

| Edit | Render tiles | Crossings and nodes | Resources | Line profiles | City demand |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Geometry of an edge no line uses (PI, radius, level) | tiles of old and new extent | re-test that edge in the grid | that edge's | none | none |
| Same, on an edge lines use | same | same | that edge's | lines using it (edge-to-lines index) | cities where their times change |
| Split an edge (new junction or station) | near the node | the node | the new junction's moves | paths rewritten; times unchanged | none (a station with no line stops serves no one) |
| Delete an edge | old extent | its nodes | its | lines using it reroute or break | their cities |
| Station placed, moved, platform length | near it | the node | its platform | lines stopping there | that city (access changes) |
| Line stops or path | none | none | resources on old and new path | that line, then others whose delay changed | cities it stops in |
| Line trains per period | none | none | resources on its path | that line, then others whose delay changed | cities whose lines changed |

- **Render tiles**: a fixed mercator grid at zoom 12 (~7 km at New York). Each tile's track buffer
  is rebuilt only when an edge in it changes (SPEC 7).
- **Propagation stops after one step**: a changed line changes loads on its resources, which change
  the delay of other lines through them, which does not change any load (5.3). So an edit costs
  the touched lines plus their neighbours at shared resources, never the whole network.
- **Demand is only woken by service changes**: a city is marked dirty when a line serving it
  changes stops or frequency, or a station-to-station time changes by 5 s or more. Drawing unused
  track never triggers a demand recompute. Recompute starts once edits pause (~0.5 s) and a newer
  edit cancels a running one (latest wins); the UI keeps the last result meanwhile (SPEC 1).
- **Money** is charged per op: the cost of new pieces minus the refund of removed ones.
- **Preview vs commit**: dragging a PI previews on the main thread; the op is sent on release.

1. **What the player builds**: double-track routes drawn as one stroke, with single track as an
   option and flying junctions as a toggle (recommended), or individual tracks drawn one by one as
   in NIMBY Rails, with flyovers built by hand?
2. **Water price**: at-grade track over water costs 5x at-grade land, but viaducts over water only
   ~1.6x their land price, so rivers get bridged rather than tunnelled (recommended). Or should
   everything over water cost 5x its land price?
