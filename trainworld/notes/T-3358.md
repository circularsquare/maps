## For Anita

**What goes wrong now.** The diagram draws the lines first. The river is then carried in afterwards with the land
around it, and nothing in that step knows which side of the river each station should be on. So where the lines'
layout moves a station a little, the river can end up on its other side. On master that's 6 of 134 riverside
stations in New York, 8 of 92 in London, 27 in Berlin, 5 in Paris and 13 in Tokyo. The three earlier fixes all tried
to repair the river after the fact, and all three made it worse (20 to 47 wrong).

### Your idea: lay the river out as a line

The river goes into the diagram's layout as if it were a line with no stops, so it gets placed at the same time as
the stations, by the same solve, instead of afterwards.

- **What it is.** Each river some line crosses becomes a chain of points along its course: one at each real bend
  that matters at the diagram's scale, plus a pair at each place a line crosses it, one just before the crossing
  and one just after. The layout moves these points the way it moves stations. It straightens the river into runs
  at 0, 45 and 90 degrees, the same as a line, and the river bends where a line would (mostly at crossings).
- **Tying it in at crossings.** For each line that crosses the river on the ground, the pair of points either side
  of the crossing is held on opposite sides of that line. So the river crosses exactly the lines it crosses on the
  ground, at about the right place along them, by construction. A line the river doesn't cross on the ground has
  no such pair, and it is kept from crossing it.
- **Tying it in at the banks.** Every station within about 1.5 km of the river on the ground is held on its own
  side of the river's chain, at least half the river's drawn width plus a little away. The layout already does this
  for stations near a long line that crosses a small city's network, so it's the same rule pointed at a new thing.
  Since the stations start on the rubber sheet, which is the real ground stretched without folding, every station
  starts on its right bank; the rule only has to keep it there.
- **How wide.** At the river's real width at the drawing's scale around it, from about three line widths up to about
  twenty, in runs of one width (the rule the old water already had). The room the layout keeps around it matches that width,
  so the band never sits on a station.
- **Its ends.** Where it reaches the sea or a lake, the band runs on into the drawn water a few line widths. Where
  the river just leaves the map, it ends a few line widths past its last crossing, as rivers do now.
- **Under parallel lines.** A line running beside the river may cover it between stations; you said that's fine.
  Its stations may not, since a station on top of the river reads as a station in the water. So the river gets pushed
  off a riverside line's stations, and in the busiest centres the layout opens a gap for it, the way the Tube map
  makes room for the Thames.
- **What the player sees.** A river drawn in the same style as the lines: straight runs and 45 degree bends,
  even width, crossing each line once at a real bridge or tunnel, with every station on its real bank. In
  dense centres the lines move apart a little to let it through.
- **Risks.** It's the biggest change: the layout has many rules that assume every point is a station (names,
  station spacing, local stop rules, the order of lines), and each has to skip river points. Lines may lose
  some straightness where the river pushes them. The bank is held by the layout's rules, not proved, so it gets
  measured. About **12 to 16 hours** to land, after a 2 hour prototype.

### Other approaches

**B. Thread the river through the gaps after the layout.** The lines stay as they are now. After they're drawn, the
river is routed through the drawing in 0, 45 and 90 degree runs, allowed to cross only the lines it crosses on the
ground. Between two crossings, the river on the ground passes through one gap between the lines (panel 4 of the
sketch), and the drawn river must use the same gap. Every station around that gap is then on the right bank
automatically: there is no way through the gap that puts it on the wrong side. Exceptions: a separate small network
sitting inside the gap, and places where the layout has squeezed a gap shut (then the river has to cross a line it
shouldn't, and that gets counted). The player sees a river that winds between the lines and narrows or hides under a
line where the gap is tight; the lines don't make room for it. Most of the router already exists from the old
water code. About **6 to 9 hours**; a 1 hour measurement first (how many gaps the drawing keeps open).

**C. Make rivers stiff when they're carried in** (the previous agent's next idea). The river is carried in with the
land as now, but made as stiff as land, so it moves with the stations around it instead of floating free. It
should cut the wrong banks, but nothing guarantees it: where the layout moves a station across the river's line, a
stiff river still ends up on the wrong side. Looks like today. About **4 to 6 hours**, mostly measuring.

**D. Keep the river where the rubber sheet puts it, and make the lines respect it.** The sheet already has every
station on the right bank. The river stays there as a fixed wall; the lines' layout keeps stations on their side of
it and puts each crossing on it. Then the river is straightened into 0, 45 and 90 degree runs within the room it was
given. It keeps more of its real shape than your idea, but in a tight centre it can't move out of the way, so the
lines bend around it instead. About **8 to 12 hours**.

### How others do it

- **Designers.** The Tube map draws the Thames as an even-width band in the same 45 and 90 degree style as the lines,
  and it is the map's main "north or south of the river" cue: TfL dropped it in September 2009 and put it back by
  December after complaints. Vignelli's 1972 New York map drew the water and coasts in the lines' geometry too. In
  both, the river is designed together with the lines, not added afterwards. That supports your idea.
- **Research.** The best known automatic method (Nöllenburg and Wolff) treats keeping the map's topology as a hard
  rule and turns every crossing into a point in the graph, which is the "crossing becomes part of the graph" half of
  your idea. Bast and Brosi's grid method routes lines on an octilinear grid and can keep them off rivers and coasts
  as obstacles, which is approach B the other way round. There is work on routing thick paths in the right "way
  around" each obstacle (homotopic routing), which is exactly the bank problem, and on simplifying a line without
  moving points to its other side. I found no paper that lays out a river as a line in a metro map.

## Detail for agents

### Where the wrong banks come from

The rubber sheet is fold-free: on the sheet every station is on its right bank of every river (the river is the
ground's, stretched). Wrong banks come in at two later steps. (1) The lines' stages (B, the snap, the routing, the
separation) move stations off their sheet places by up to several pitches with no term about rivers. (2) The carry
moves the ground to the drawn stations with water links at 0.004 against land's 5, so a narrow river goes where the
smooth field puts it, not where the stations' sides say (try 2 in T-3345 showed this: meeting stations harder does
not move the river). `riverBands` then moves the carried middle toward the crossings within 3 km (`corrAt`) and
pushes it radially off stations, which keeps whatever side it already had. Every earlier fix worked after step 2.
The prototype should count wrong banks at three points (on the sheet, which should be 0; the lines' drawing against
the river at its sheet place; the final drawing) to confirm which step does the damage.

### The river as a line: the mechanics

- **Which rivers.** City maps, as narrow rivers now: rivers from `realWater` that a hop crosses, joined into courses
  (the parked `riverCourses` in the earlier scratchpad's `river-wip/`), and that part no land mass. Rivers that do part
  land masses (the Hudson, the East River) are already safe: the carry puts every station on its own land mass. Wide
  rivers that part nothing (stretches that are area water today) are included, and their carried area water within
  the band's reach goes to land, as `riverBands` does now.
- **Nodes.** In `sheetLines`, before `layoutDiagram`: the course on the sheet (`sheetWarp`) simplified at about one
  pitch, plus a pair of nodes about a pitch either side of each crossing. Added to `g2` as nodes with a flag (a new
  `river` per node, or keys below a fixed negative base: river id times 4096 plus index, stable across rebuilds), and
  one `DiagramLine` per course with a `river` flag, `stop` all 0, drawn by nobody.
- **Crossings without splitting hops.** Planarising (a dummy node on the hop, as Nöllenburg and Wolff do) would make
  it a degree-4 node, which `segments()` makes a key node (`ns.length !== 2`), splitting the hop into two pieces that
  can bend at the river. Instead the pair either side of a crossing is held on opposite sides of the hop at its
  share, in the existing `crossSides` form (e, v, share, side), so the hop's own piece is untouched. The river edge
  between the pair is short, so it crosses the hop once.
- **Banks.** Stations within `CROSS_SIDE_M` (1.5 km) of the course on the ground, against each river edge, in the same
  form, with the river's drawn half width in place of `P.hw[v]`, and **not** skipped in dense places (crossTies skips
  places with 12 stations within 2.5 km; the river is what bends there, not the lines). `crossSideTerms` runs in B and
  the snap only; the separation needs the same linear inequality (it is already Hildreth projections of linearised
  terms, so it fits), else the routing and separation can undo it.
- **Exclusions.** River nodes must be left out of: half widths and the spacing of unconnected stations
  (`closePairs`, `sidePairs`, the separation's push of stations off other legs, except against stations), `cityParts`
  and the lonely and local hop rules, the sea and water-gap pairs, `crossTies`, `order`, names and city names, pills,
  the station counts in checks. The river's own edges get octilinear terms and a target of their sheet length. River
  legs may overlap other lines' legs (hidden under, accepted); the bank terms keep them off stations.
- **Drawing.** The river line's routed polyline is the band's middle: widths as the old Water "How wide" rule (real
  width at the scale field, 3 lines' width to 18 pitches, runs of one width), corners rounded at 2.5 pitches. Ends: a
  mouth runs on in its direction to the nearest drawn cell of its body's sea within about 10 pitches, crossing no line;
  an open or free end stops 3 pitches past its last crossing (5 for a wide river).
- **Cost.** London has 864 stations; its crossed rivers would add perhaps 100 to 300 nodes. The layout is about 0.6 of
  `sheetLines`' time, so perhaps 10 to 30% more there. `riverBands` and its carry of the river go.
- **Checks to keep.** t2580-sheet-water (0/45/90 edges, no stairs, stations on land and under no band, the Harlem River
  drawn), the wrong-bank check below, smoke.

### B, threading through the gaps: the mechanics

On the drawing's grid (the `sheetMap` grid), every hop's casing is a wall except the hops the river crosses on the
ground, which are open only at the crossing's run (at the hop's share, as the old Water rule places it). Between
consecutive crossings along the course, route on a one-pitch grid with water.ts's costs (`CELL`, `B45` 10, `B90` 30,
the corridor near the carried course), walls impassable, pills impassable. Why that gives the bank: between two
crossings the ground river lies in one face of the planar network (hop crossings as vertices); in a simply connected
face every path between the same two boundary points splits the face's boundary the same way, so every station on
that boundary is on its right bank. Dangling branches are part of the boundary walk, so they are fine. Breaks: a
separate network inside the face (a hole, decide its side by a test), and a face the drawing pinched shut (no path;
cross the cheapest wall and count it). This is Speckmann and Verbeek's homotopic routing with the stations as point
obstacles, done on a grid. A 1 hour measurement: label the drawing's faces by flood fill of the non-casing cells and
count, per river, consecutive crossings whose crossing runs open into one drawn face.

### A better check

The parked `t3358-river-sides.mjs` compares a station's side of the nearest band piece, which is noisy where a band
is in pieces or wiggles. A cleaner count: for each pair of stations within about 3 km of each other and of a drawn
river, the parity of crossings of the river's middle on the straight way between them, on the ground and in the
drawing; a pair whose parity differs is split wrongly. It needs no "nearest piece", and it counts any river in any
approach the same way.

### First step (the prototype, about 2 hours)

In a scratchpad: a wrapper around `sheetLines` that adds the river chains and side terms to `g2` (in a copy of
layout.ts if the exclusions need it; at first river nodes may simply get half width 0), lays out New York (the real
save) and London, and draws the river lines as bands into a debug PNG of each centre (the Harlem River and Newtown
Creek; the Thames from Battersea to Greenwich). Reports: wrong banks by the parked check and the pair check, at the
three points above; the share of hops drawn as one straight run (New York 86% today); layout time. Go on with the
river as a line if wrong banks are 0 in New York and London (or each one left is a station the lines' stages already
put across a line) and the straight share drops at most 3 points; else B.

## The gap measurement, as tried

Per course, every crossing of any hop's track (not the finder's odd-count list), in order; for consecutive crossings
over 150 m apart, the face beside each crossing (stepping out from the drawn hop at its share, the side the river goes
to, past small slivers) on a raster of the drawing with every casing and pill as wall. On the sheet (tracks warped,
thin walls) this should be 100%. It gave 25 of 35 and 15 of 30, so the probe is wrong in some cases: likely a share of
a warped or drawn hop landing past a bend or beside a station, probes jumping a nearby parallel hop, or a river
crossing near a hop's end. Not debugged.
