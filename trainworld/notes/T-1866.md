# T-1866: a destination bubble's homes, a few big and many tiny (2026-10-30)

Anita: "why is it that when we select a destination demand bubble, a common pattern is like a
bunch of paired homes that have like 2k people (or some constant number) and then lots of other
tiny dots that are like 10 people? feels a bit weird. why dont we just have one or the other?"

## Plain answer

The two kinds of dot come from two different kinds of trip, which the game works out in two
different ways.

The tiny dots are commutes. Commutes are worked out between 2 km squares and then spread over
every home in each square by how many people live there, so a job in Midtown draws a little from
almost every home in the city: about 29,000 homes, three quarters of them under 10 trips each.
Since T-909 the map groups those slivers into dots of at least 10, which is why they all look
about 10.

The big dots are everyday trips (school and university above all, also shopping and visits). To
keep demand fast, the game doesn't spread these over every home. It picks a handful of example
homes and gives each one an equal share. A school or university picks up to 16 homes for its
students. Columbia's 40,000 trip legs a day go to exactly 16 homes at 2,521 each; NYU's 64,000
to 16 homes at 4,017 each (two of them landed on the same home, so that bubble shows 8,035).
That is your "constant number". They come in pairs because the picks are spread evenly over the
nearby squares, and the busiest square near the campus often gets two picks, which land side by
side. Shopping and visits work the same way with smaller shares (a home's errands split 16 ways),
which gives the mid-sized dots, 100-2,000, around a Midtown destination.

So the totals are right, but where the big dots sit is a random pick standing in for "students
come from all around here". It is a side effect of how the trips are sampled, shown at a finer
level than it means. Real students would show up as many smaller dots, thicker near the campus,
like the commutes.

The fix I'd suggest only changes the drawing: draw the everyday trips' homes as the smooth spread
the samples stand for (each home's fair share), scaled so the totals stay exactly the same. Then a
destination's homes are all one kind: thin everywhere, thicker nearby. Demand itself doesn't
change. A second fix changes a demand rule, so it needs your OK: give big schools and universities
more sample homes (one per 32 trips instead of at most 16). That makes Columbia about 630 homes
of 64 legs instead of 16 of 2,521, and it makes the station loads near big campuses a bit more
accurate too. It costs about 3% more samples in New York.

## Where each kind comes from (code)

- **Commutes** (`sim/src/demand/api.rs` `flows_day`): the gravity's 2 km zone trips from the
  selection, spread over every cell of each far zone by people (`tot = t_z[j] * fw[c] / fzone[j]`),
  rail from the far subzones; the smallest cells holding the last 1% are cut. Smooth and thin.
- **Everyday purposes** (`sim/src/demand/everyday.rs` `selection_parts`, asked as
  `selection_sparse_day` by the demand worker's `purposes` message): the far end is the sample
  edges themselves, each sample's whole weight on the one cell it drew. Samples per fixed cell:
  `lod.rs` `Sampling::FINE`, one per 8 trips a day, 2 to 16 (2 for airport, medical and
  attractions). Weight `budget * rate / per * local_share(distance) * kept` (`EverydayApi::weight`);
  `local_share` is 1 up to 40 km, so every sample of one school or university carries exactly the
  same figure. School and university draw their samples from the site (`site_anchored`), so a
  selected campus has at most 16 home cells. Outings draw from the home: a home cell with one of
  its 16 samples in the selection shows a sixteenth of its errands (or visits).
- **Pairs**: `Sampler::sample` places a fixed cell's samples at evenly spaced points of the zone
  CDF ((k + offset) / n), then a random cell inside the zone; a zone holding over 1/16 of the mass
  (the one round the campus) gets two samples, which land in two cells of the same 2 km square, or
  the same cell (NYU's 8,035 = 2 x 4,017).
- **The ~10 dots**: `app/src/game/farChunks.ts` (T-909) deals far cells under 10 trips into groups
  of at least 10 along a Hilbert curve; the commutes' 27-29k sub-10 cells become 1,000-3,200 dots of
  10-20. Far bubbles are sized among themselves (`commuterView.ts` `drawPick`: the biggest is a
  hexagon's area), so beside a 2,500 lump a 10 looks like a speck.

## Real or drawn?

Both, in parts. The totals are the model's: the selection does get these trips by purpose and
mode. The lumps are the model's own integration, too: demand assigns the 16 sample homes' trips to
the network (through their subzone pairs), so a campus's riders really do start from 16 places
in the solve. But a sample stands for "a share of the trips from around here", and the far end
draws it on one res-9 cell, finer than it means. The commutes sit at the other extreme (every
cell, thinly), so two honest answers side by side look like two different kinds of thing.

## Proposed fixes (none built)

**1. Draw the everyday far end as its expected spread (drawing only; recommended).** For each
purpose, replace the sample cells with what the samples estimate: for a selected site (school,
university) the sampler's own home probabilities (zone mass x distance decay, then cell weight);
for outings into a selected destination, each home cell's trips times its chance of drawing a
destination in the selection (local and central draws). Scale each purpose to its sampled total,
so the near end, the hover, the explorer and the far end still add up exactly; the mode split per
far cell from the selection's own split for that purpose (or its subzone pair's where known).
Then commutes and everyday trips look alike: thin everywhere, thick near the destination, and
T-909's grouping does the rest.
- Cost: one new `EverydayApi` query; it needs each purpose's zone masses and cell weights kept after
  opening (zones x 7 purposes plus cells x 7 floats: about 2 MB for New York) and each home zone's
  normaliser; a few ms per selection (home zones x selected zones). No demand time, no change to
  any result. T-586's agreement check re-run (the explorer reads the same far end).
- The lie: the far bubbles show the model's expectation, not the exact sample homes the loads
  were assigned from; a far bubble's mode split is approximate. Station loads are unchanged.

A smaller drawing-only step: spread each sample over its own 2 km square's cells by people
before the grouping (about 20 lines in `workers/demandSums.ts` `flowsBody`, from `cell_zone` and
`cell_commuters`, both already exposed). It removes the single-cell spikes and the pairs at street
zooms, but zoomed out (res 7, about a 2 km square per bubble) Columbia would still be 16 blobs of
2,500. It doesn't answer the question, so I'd not do it alone.

**2. More sample homes for big schools and universities (a demand rule change: Anita's call).**
Site-anchored purposes take one sample per 32 trips a day with no cap of 16 (small sites keep
2-16 as now). Columbia: 16 samples of 2,521 legs become about 630 of 64; NYU about 1,000.
- Cost in New York: school and university now hold at most 4,412 x 16 + 261 x 16 = 74.8k samples;
  under the rule about 76k (school, 2.42M trips / 32) plus 15.7k (university, 502k / 32), so roughly
  +30k on about a million everyday samples: about 3% more opening time and pairs, about 250 KB.
- What it changes in demand: the rail near big campuses comes from many homes instead of 16
  (station loads near Columbia, NYU, CUNY colleges move; citywide totals barely). It also fixes the
  lumps on the Homes side for a selected campus. It leaves shopping and visits' smaller lumps
  (a home's errands split 16 ways), which only fix 1 removes; more samples for those would cost
  real demand time (they are most of the samples).

Fix 1 answers the question everywhere; fix 2 is worth considering on its own for campus loads.
