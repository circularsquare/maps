## How it was measured

Headless Chrome pinned to 6 of the machine's 16 cores (mask 0xFC00), 3 solving workers unless a row says
otherwise (what `poolSize` gives a desktop with 12 or more threads; laptops with 6-11 threads get 2).
Instrumentation in the private tree only: each population's solve, crowding, capacity-fit and set-up calls timed
in the workers, the client's messages timed on the page, 50-100 ms sampling of the status tag and the lines'
"calculating..." label. A second build of the WASM with function names, used only for the profile section. Saves:
`T-007-real-nyc.save` (109 lines, 1,756 stations), Tokyo day 414 (70 lines, 1,041 stations, 20 zones reached, 18
of them coarse), North America day 476 (158 lines, 2,115 stations, 113 zones, 104 coarse), Anita's own files in
Downloads, read only.

**Noise.** Other agents and a rustc were on the machine throughout. Repeated cold loads spread 64-99 s on NYC
and 59-110 s on Tokyo, so read any single number as +-30%, and the comparisons below as the pattern across
repeats, not to the second. Medians of 1-2 runs. Downloads here are local (Vite, disk cache warm), so what a
real connection adds is not in any figure.

The camera sits on the edited line's middle station at zoom 11 before each edit, as a player's would.

**A cold load** below is a reload with the browser's file cache warm but the demand cache missed, which is what
every player gets after each deploy (the key has the build) and what a first visit gets once the packs are
downloaded. A cached reload (a returning player, same build) is measured separately.

## The two finish lines an edit has

Every edit goes through two stages, and the player sees them differently (T-1733):

1. The **local solve**: only the zones the edit touches, everything else frozen. When it lands the edited
   line's "calculating..." label clears, the zone's figures update, and the status tag ("Trips here being
   recomputed...") goes, if the player is looking at that zone. For most edits this is the wait they feel.
2. The **whole solve**: starts 8 s after the local result (`EDIT_SETTLE_MS`), re-solves the cluster with all its
   populations and the regional market, replaces everything when it lands. No tag shows in the zone in view
   during it (only for a zone still being solved elsewhere); the network totals and other zones' figures change
   when it lands, and a next edit made once it is under way waits for it (T-1734's "firm" rule).

Below, **local** is edit to the zone's own figures with label and tag gone; **final** is edit to the last
demand figures (`demandUpdating` off, everything current).

What's in those numbers:

- Most of the time is the solve itself. Before it starts, downloads and pack opening take 10-17 s on NYC, 19-31 s
  on Tokyo and 74 s on North America (local server, warm file cache: no real download in these).
- Round 0 costs more than the later rounds (NYC 19-26 s against 11-13 s). About 5 s of that is one `figures` call in
  each worker at the end of round 0 (4.8-4.9 s, in parallel), which builds the market's corridor figures for the first time;
  the market's first free-flow solve and the first `setUp` are the rest, and the focus pool beside it costs CPU too.
- Set-up is large on the biggest save: North America 19-26 s per worker before round 0 (NYC 1.6-3.6, Tokyo 2-6),
  almost all `IntercityApi.set_network_ll` plus each city's `set_network`.
- The main thread is not the problem: 36-715 ms of the page's message handling in a whole load, 15 ms typical
  queue delay. Nothing to gain there.

**Cached reload.** NYC: figures at 3.5 s, game running at 11.4 s, and then the tag says "Trips in 4 cities being
recomputed..." until 73 s while the main pool re-solves the same network so later clicks and edits have worker
state. Tokyo: figures at 5.8 s, running at 9.9 s, tag gone at 31 s, the re-solve ends at 81 s. The figures shown
are final from the first frame; nothing the tag says is true of them, so a returning player reads a minute of
"recomputing" that changes nothing they see.

## Where the time goes

**The whole wait is CPU in the workers, and passes are most of it.** NYC, one edit (`dropCity`), named WASM,
all workers, 203 s of work: the commute pair loop (`mode_choice_part`) 47% inclusive (36% self), strategy
search (`skims_extend`) 22%, everyday pass 14%, market pass 15%, assignment 5%. The pair loop is mostly as
T-401 found natively (37% then; the search was 12% natively then, not a like-for-like figure). `figures`, `keepSums`, `setUp`
together are a few percent of a whole solve on NYC and Tokyo (2-3 s) and 6-9 s on North America.

**Why NYC's local solve is 42 s and Tokyo's 6.** A local solve re-solves the edited zone's own populations
through every round of every period. On NYC that zone is nearly the whole game: one very dense network (tens of
millions of commute pairs a pass, T-401), one pass 4-5.5 s, and a period is the free-flow pass, three crowding
passes and a capacity fit, about 25-30 s for the two peaks. The local solve saves NYC only the market and three small
zones. Tokyo's edited zone passes in about 0.3 s, so its local solve is 4-6 s.

**The local solve waits on its slowest worker.** Three workers on five periods: the worker holding the morning
and evening peaks (0 and 3) took 40-49 s against 30-36 s for the others (NYC). Taking more cores is the only
thing that shortens that: 5 workers (8 cores) 28-38 s against 41-48. With 2 workers it was 50 s, not 65, because
more workers do little when a period is the unit of parallel work and two periods sit on one worker either way.

**The whole solve waits for the settle gap, then repeats most of the work.** 8 s (`EDIT_SETTLE_MS`) plus each
worker waiting for the slowest local solve (`waitWhole` 13-27 s), then the whole solve 33-78 s, even though the
local solve just solved the two populations that changed: the whole solve has to set the rest of the cluster
on the new network and let the crowding and the market answer, and that is the expensive part (the market
alone was about 1.7 s a period on NYC, but 3-6 s a period on North America).

**Round by round.** Crowding rounds are not cheap: every round repeats search, mode choice and assignment. A
period that has settled (T-434: its loads moved under 1%) stops early, which is why some periods cost 1 pass and
others 4. In the NYC runs the off-peak periods stop early and the peaks don't.

**Two places on the biggest save with waste that isn't work.** Both are on North America, 113 zones, and worth
naming on their own:

- The market's **window** (T-1735) on an edit of a line whose changed run reaches 20 minutes: a fresh `IntercityApi`
  beside the market gets its whole setup every time, 13.3-13.5 s of `set_network_ll` in each worker (three drop
  or new-line edits in North America; none on New York or Tokyo, which no edit of a city line triggered).
  That is half of a 27-s local solve. The market part next to it (set up for the whole solve right after) takes
  0.7 s. The rebuild is the served-pair and zone tables built from nothing for the new population. If the sub
  could start from the parent's tables and update only for what the edit changed, the 13 s would go
  (estimate, from these span timings; the idea has not been built).
- Set-up before a game load's first round: 19-26 s in each worker (`setUp`, almost all `set_network_ll` for 113 zones
  of market) that a load always pays. Same family as the point above; a load has to build it once.

## Looking outward

What comparable tools do to keep edits quick, and why it helps little here:

- **NIMBY Rails** precalculates demand between map tiles in the background and re-runs the passenger pathfinder on
  each line edit (devblog posts [2024-05](https://carloscarrasco.com/page/16/), [2024-06](https://carloscarrasco.com/page/15/), [2022-07](https://carloscarrasco.com/page/38/)). It is quadratic and slow on edits to stations, so it pays once, in the background. Passengers in the
  sim follow the fastest routes; it has no crowding loop to re-run, which is the expensive part here.
- **Cities: Skylines II** has route choice per agent (time, comfort, money, behaviour costs) and uses patches to
  remove redundant pathfinding ([traffic AI](https://www.paradoxinteractive.com/games/cities-skylines-ii/features/traffic-ai), [patch notes](https://www.paradoxinteractive.com/games/cities-skylines-ii/news/patch-notes-morning-dew)); it has no aggregate assignment to re-solve.
- **Transit assignment engines.** The usual method is a frequency-based assignment with crowding, solved by
  repeated assignment and averaging (the method of successive averages, MSA), which is known to converge slowly
  ([TMG's Emme toolbox documents 100 iterations and gap criteria](https://tmg.utoronto.ca/doc/2.0/tmgtoolbox2_emme/tools/Assign/AssignTransit.html); [gap-based methods beat MSA on Chicago](https://www.worldtransitresearch.info/research/6031)). Our three crowding rounds are very few by those standards, which is why the figures are not settled and fewer rounds move lines by 5-14%
  (measured below).
- **Warm starts and incremental updates.** Road and dynamic assignment have well-studied warm starts: reusing the
  previous equilibrium and re-solving only what a change disturbs, usually with a proper gap measure
  ([warm-started DTA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.03570), [recursive logit warm start with a list of changed parts](https://transp-or.epfl.ch/heart/2020/abstracts/HEART_2020_paper_33.pdf), [perturbation assignment](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/23815/dot_23815_DS1.pdf)). A warm start would suit this model, but three rounds from free flow give a figure that
  depends on the path taken (T-1733's finding), so a warm start would shift every line a few percent once, and then keep it there.
  A cheaper version of the same idea is already in use: the local solve (T-1733).
- **Road-network shortcuts** ([Customizable Contraction Hierarchies](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.10519)): their
  gain is on route search with changing weights, and search is only 22% of a pass here.
- **WASM.** SIMD is already on (T-036). Threads in one worker would need cross-origin isolation headers
  (COOP/COEP) on the site and its data hosts; see "Ruled out" (not a proposal).

Sources found, none of them an answer as such: no game or engine publishes per-edit timings for an assignment like
this one. The closest match to this model is professional transit assignment software, which runs for minutes per
scenario and has no edit loop.

## What could shorten it

Ranked within each group by what a player would feel. "Measured" means these runs; "estimated" means worked out from
them. Sizes are rough, for one agent.

### A. Doing the same work faster, or skipping repeats (no figure a player could tell is wrong)

**A1. Let the next edit go without waiting behind a whole solve that has only just started.** Now, once the
whole solve is running (from about 8 s after the local result), any new edit waits for it to end, then for its own
local solve. Whole solves here are 33-78 s, so an edit made in that window shows nothing for a minute or two. Instead:
when a whole solve has done less than a set share of its work (say the first quarter), a new edit cancels and
restarts it; cap the cancels (one or two) so steady building still lands.

Measured, one edit made 10 s after the previous edit's label cleared (a headway change on the save's longest line): on New York its figures came 97 s after that edit (a lone edit: 41-49 s), on Tokyo 57 s (6-7 s alone), on North America 82 s (17-45 s alone). So an edit that lands during a whole solve waits 50-65 s longer than it needs to.
Risk: edits arriving quickly cost CPU spent re-doing whole solves that never land: bounded by the cap, and steady
building keeps T-1734's guarantee (a whole solve past the cap, or past that first quarter, runs on as now).
Size: small to medium, client only (`flush`, `firm`). Saving: most of the wait behind the whole solve, 30-75 s.

**A2. Share the market window's tables with the market part beside it.** Do not rebuild the served pairs and tables
for the T-1735 window from nothing; start from the parent's. Saves 13 s of a North America local solve on every edit
of a line whose changed run is at least 20 minutes (measured: 13.3-13.5 s of 17-45 s local). No effect on New York
and Tokyo here. Risk none if the copy is exact (a bit check against today's result, like T-2653's). Size: small to medium, Rust.

**A3. More workers on machines that can hold them.** 3 workers on a 16-thread desktop is a choice from T-381 for
memory and sharing CPU. With 5 (one per period) the NYC local solve is 28-38 s against 41-48 s and the final
76-95 s against 106-120 s (measured, one run each, 8 cores against 6, so part of it). Gain only for machines with
memory to spare: each solving worker holds 367 MB on NYC and 1.1 GB on North America, so 5 workers is 5.5 GB in one
tab there. T-2867 (Firefox out of memory on a 16 GB machine) says do this with care. Proposal: 4-5 workers only with 16+ threads
and enough `deviceMemory` for the save, sized by the worker's own `need` figure; otherwise as now. Risk: more fan
noise, more CPU taken from the player's other windows, memory. Size: small.

**A4. Shorten the settle gap.** `EDIT_SETTLE_MS` (8 s) buys a window for a second edit to go locally before the whole
solve starts; 3 s would keep most of that (line-then-trains edits arrive within a couple of seconds in play), saving
5 s of every final (estimated, directly from the constant). Risk: an edit 4-8 s after a local result joins the
firm wait rather than going locally (A1 covers it). Size: tiny.

**A5. The tag on a cached reload.** A returning player sees figures at 3.5 s (NYC) and "Trips in 4 cities being
recomputed..." for the next minute, about something they can see is already there. Either hide the tag while a
cached result is the view, or word it for what is happening. Not a speedup, but it is a minute of "calculating" taken
off most game opens (measured: 11 s to 73 s on NYC, 10 s to 31 s on Tokyo). Risk: none to the figures. Size: tiny, her wording call.

**A6. Lazy bubbles' sums at a whole solve's end.** At the final every zone's sums are worked out and sent, whether
or not it is on screen (`keepSums`: 6-9 s per worker on North America; 2-3 s on NYC and Tokyo). A zone asked for later
could be answered from the workers, who are idle after the final. Saves up to about 5 s of a North America final
(estimated). Risk: a later ask for a zone off screen costs a moment. Size: small.

### B. Approximations a player could see

**B1. A one-period preview of an edit's local solve.** Solve the morning peak first (one period, as T-1538 does
at free flow), estimate the rest, show that at once, then replace it with the real local result. NYC: figures in
about 10 s instead of 44 (estimated: 1 of 5 periods plus pair-loop overhead). Lie: the other four periods are
estimated; T-1538 measured the free-flow case at +2 to +7% for most periods and +19-29% for night and for a
fifth of the day's trips (peaks are near-exact). Numbers would move twice after an edit (estimate, then local result,
then whole), so "calculating" would feel shorter but the figures visibly refine. Would need a rule like T-2793's for what not to show.
Size: small to medium.

**B2. Two crowding rounds instead of three, everywhere.** A quarter fewer passes in every solve and edit (estimated;
a run with 2 rounds finishes its load earlier by about a round's time, noisy here). This is the only single
change that shortens the local wait, the whole solve and the load together. Measured against 3 rounds:
network riders +0.1%, but New York 13 of 92 lines over 10,000 riders past 5%, 1 past 10%, worst -14%; Tokyo 3 of 68
past 5%, worst +7.5%. The figures are not settled either way (T-1733), so a player sees no wrongness as such, only
that lines shift once when it ships, and a save loaded before and after differs. Her two ceiling tests would need
checking again. One round: worst 21%, ruled out (below). Size: tiny (a constant), a calibration call for her.

**B3. Coarser commute pair chunks in a local solve.** Commute zone pairs under 0.1 trips a period are already dealt
in chunks (T-874, -17-19% browser edit time, stations 0.1%). A larger chunk (0.3) for the local solve alone, the whole
solve keeping 0.1, might save another 10-15% of the local (estimated from T-874 and T-869's -33% at 0.1 pruned; not tested).
Lie: thin commutes averaged across subzones in the edited zone until the whole solve lands, seconds later. Risk: noise near
small stations. Size: small.

**B4. Treat a small local result as final** (keep the whole solve, but don't re-solve the populations that the local
solve already solved in it, or defer the whole solve while the player keeps building). Saves part of the whole solve
but T-1733 measured the local result missing the market's response on long new lines, and A1 gets most of what a player
would feel at no risk. Not recommended unless A1 falls short.

## Ruled out

- **Fewer rounds than two** (one round: worst line 21%, 34 of 92 NYC lines past 5%).
- **Splitting a period over workers beyond the free-flow preview** (T-1543's parts): each part repeats the station
  searches, so per pass the parts are not much under half a whole; for all 20 passes of a NYC local solve that comes
  out no faster than giving each worker whole periods (worked out from these timings).
- **Recomputing only the pairs an edit touches.** Crowding feedback changes every pair every round, and per-pair
  caches would be about 76 MB a period; no.
- **Cutting the network into pieces** (T-1650 already; geographic cuts would need the market to be split).
- **Moving anything off the main thread.** 15 ms typical; the page is not the bottleneck.
- **WASM threads inside a worker.** SIMD is already on; shared-memory threads need cross-origin isolation (COOP/COEP) on the
  site and data hosts, a real porting job for a share of a pass (the pair loop) this per-period parallel work already gets most of.
- **The T-124 period reuse** is already in and removes whole periods on train-count edits; not revisited.

## Not measured

- A real connection (downloads, the R2 fetches): every cold load here read packs from local disk, so a first visit's
  download is on top of these figures (perf-survey's numbers suggest tens of seconds at modest bandwidth on a big save).
- A real Firefox or Safari, a laptop, a phone. Everything here is one Windows desktop in Chrome.
- The real North America cached reload (only NYC and Tokyo were measured).
- Interacting while solving (a bubble click during a whole solve), only whole edits and loads.
- A pure "add a stop" with a newly built station. "addBack" re-adds a dropped stop on an existing station, close but
  not identical to the build path.
- The accuracy of B1 and B3, which were not built.
- Fare edits beyond one run on Tokyo (21 s local, 58 s final). The first New York and Tokyo runs set the fare wrong by a script mistake (a flat base over her curve), so those fare edits and the edits after them were thrown away; New York and North America have no valid fare edit.
- A2 and A1 are not built, so their savings are the time spent in those steps today, not a before-and-after.
