# T-2668: fares by country, evidence and a better default (2026-11-06, agent farescale)

Research and proposal only: no code or data changed. Scripts, the fare table and the test lines are
in `notes/T-2668/` (they ran from a scratch folder; paths inside point there).

## For Anita

- **Your memory is right.** In T-1277 (2026-10-25) you picked fares and the value of time scaling
  with the square root of income, measured at PPP. T-1521 (2026-10-27) switched the game to plain US
  dollars at market rates, and the same rule written against dollar income is a power of 0.7. Prices
  in poorer countries are lower, and that difference is what turns 0.5 into 0.7. It has been the same
  rule all along.
- **Real fares scale about that much.** Across 46 cities on every continent, a short metro ride costs
  about income^0.66 (90% range 0.56-0.75). The game's default fares sit on that line. Your two examples:
  Mumbai's metro is about ₹20 ($0.23), and Tokyo Metro is ¥180 ($1.20). That is 5x apart for 13x the
  income, a power of 0.64.
- **Tijuana loses money because of costs, not fares.** Fares follow the country, but running costs
  and upkeep are one world price list. A Tijuana rider pays a quarter of a San Diego rider, while a car
  running a km costs the same in both. To break even, a train needs on average 4.6 riders a car in New
  York, 8.7 in Tokyo, 17 in Tijuana and 53 in Mumbai. My test line through Tijuana carries 1.8x the
  riders of a similar line in San Diego and earns 0.45x the fares. Run as a busy line would need (6-car
  trains every 2.5 minutes), it loses $10.3M a game day.
- **The default is the same sensible setting everywhere today.** The fare scale and the value of time
  use the same power, so a default fare feels the same to riders in every country (about 11 minutes for
  the base fare). On both test lines the default is about 40-50% of the fare that earns most. It brings
  in 78-86% of that best income and carries about twice the riders. I would keep that.
- **The value of time can come down a little, not a lot.** Measured on real income, the evidence puts
  it at 0.5-1 across countries and 0.1-0.4 among transit riders within one country. Today's 0.7 in
  dollars is 0.59 on real income. 0.6 in dollars (0.46) is still supported, and 0.5 (0.32) only by the
  within-country studies. Lowering it gives poorer countries more riders at the default fares (Tijuana
  +8%, Mumbai +15% at 0.6) and a little more income, but it does not make a busy line pay.
- **My recommendation:** keep fares at 0.7. Charge running costs and upkeep at each country's fare
  scale, so Mexico pays 0.27x US prices, India 0.09x and Japan 0.53x. Construction and trains stay at
  world prices. If you want the value of time lower, use 0.6. The busy Tijuana line goes from -$10.3M to
  +$6.1M a game day, the lighter one from +$1.7M to +$8.9M, and New York stays exactly as it is. The
  cost change alone touches nothing in demand. The value-of-time change is a demand change, and it is
  your call.

## 1. History: how 0.7 came about

- **T-1277, round 1** (2026-10-25, `notes/archive/t1000-1499/T-1277.md`): with a fare dollar worth a
  flat 3 minutes everywhere, the fare that earned most was silly (about 6x the starting fares, $16 an
  average trip). The agent proposed 9 minutes a dollar. You found that too steep and asked for a weight
  by national income instead (DECISIONS 2026-10-25).
- **T-1277, round 2**: the agent proposed minutes a fare dollar = 5 x (GDP per head at PPP /
  $60,000)^-0.7 (HEATCO's 0.7; the UK meta-analysis has 0.9, European appraisal practice 0.5-1). It also
  proposed charging fares in local terms at the same power, so that the income term cancels and every
  country weighs the curve alike. **You chose the gentler 0.5** (DECISIONS 2026-10-25: "a fare dollar's
  weight follows national income with the gentler exponent 0.5"), with a fare scale per country by default
  from income at the same 0.5.
- **T-1521, round 2** (2026-10-27, `notes/archive/t1500-1999/T-1521.md`): game dollars became US
  dollars at market rates (a PPP dollar would show: Beijing - Shanghai at $158 on an $83 ticket). To keep
  every country's minutes per unit of its own money as they were, the power on market-rate GDP became
  **0.7** for both the weight (4.4 minutes a dollar at $90,000) and the fare scale (income over India's,
  to the 0.7). The note says 0.7 "is also HEATCO's elasticity and T-1277's first proposal". It fit
  T-1521's ten-country fare table as well as PPP had.
- **T-1670** (2026-10-28): the default curve became one straight line; the scales were not touched.
- **T-1906** (2026-11-02): a new game's curve is set from where it starts (US $2.50) and every other
  country defaults to its own income's fares at the 0.7. DECISIONS records you then: "0.7 is better,
  maybe even lower, but not linear".

So the 0.7 on dollar income is your T-1277 "square root" on real income, carried over to market dollars.

## 2b. Evidence: the value of time against income

The game's minutes a fare dollar, 4.4 x (GDP per head / $90,000)^-0.7, is the inverse of a value of
time in market dollars that rises with dollar income at 0.7. Across all 239 countries in
`countryIncome.json`, GDP at PPP goes as market GDP to the 0.736 (`ppp.mjs`), so a value of time rising
with real (PPP) income at e rises with dollar income at 0.26 + 0.74e:

| Power on dollar income | Same on real income | Supported by |
|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 1.0 | Wardman, Chintakayala and de Jong 2016 (3,109 European values): country means against GDP at PPP 0.98-1.10 by purpose, the full model 1.03 (±13%); usual appraisal practice (US DOT, World Bank: in line with wages) |
| 0.78 | 0.7 | HEATCO (EU guidance) |
| **0.7 (today)** | **0.59** | Wardman et al. 2016 against GDP per head not at PPP: 0.72 (±15%); Shires and de Jong 2009 international meta-analysis: 0.47 business, 0.67 commuting, 0.52 other (as quoted by Wardman et al.; basis not stated there) |
| **0.6** | **0.46** | the lower end of the meta-analyses; Binsuwadan et al. 2023 within-country studies between people: typically about 0.5 |
| 0.5 | 0.32 | within-country studies of transit riders only: Binsuwadan et al. 2023 Table 5, public transport, between people, 0.11-0.37 (0.01-0.54 over their income range); Börjesson and Eliasson 2018 0.17 for public transport (0.5 for car) |
| 0.26 | 0 | nobody: the same real value of time at every income |

- The most direct cross-country evidence (meta-analyses across countries) says the value of time
  rises with real income at about 0.5-1, so 0.6-1.0 on dollar income. **Today's 0.7 is already on the
  gentle side of it**, which was your T-1277 choice.
- **0.6 is supported** (Shires and de Jong's range; within-country studies between people). **0.5 is
  supported only by the within-country figures for transit riders**, which compare people facing the
  same prices in one country. They are a weak guide to how a Mumbai rider weighs money against a New
  Yorker. Below 0.5 I found nothing.
- Binsuwadan et al. also find the elasticity is larger at low incomes (0.51 mean for incomes under
  $20,000 against 0.31-0.35 above $40,000), so poorer countries are, if anything, more fare-sensitive.

## 3. Why a busy Tijuana line loses money

**The rules** (`docs/track-and-service.md` 6.4): fares are the curve times the country's fare scale
(Mexico 3.1, the US 11.6: 0.27x). Construction ($75M a km on a viaduct), trains ($2.5M a car),
running costs ($4 a train-km + $2 a car-km) and upkeep ($25k a viaduct km and $50k a station a game
day) are one price list worldwide. **Nothing on the cost side scales by country.**

**Measured** (`gen_line.mjs`, `run.py`, `options.mjs`): the native `calib` (built 2026-11-04) on a test
line in an otherwise empty network, commutes and everyday trips, three crowding rounds, the regional
market off. **Tijuana**: Centro, Zona Río, Agua Caliente, 5 y 10, La Mesa, Las Américas, La Presa, El
Florido. It is 16.6 km with 15 stations on the `tij` pack, at Mexico's default fares ($0.65 + 5.5 cents
a km) and 16.3 minutes a dollar. **San Diego** for comparison: downtown, Hillcrest, North Park, City
Heights, College Area, toward La Mesa. It is 15.7 km with 14 stations on `snd`, at $2.50 + 20 cents a
km and 4.4 minutes a dollar. Both are on a viaduct at 40 km/h with 30 s stops. "Light" is 4-car trains
every 5 minutes 6-20 and every 10 minutes 20-24. "Busy" is 6-car trains every 2.5 and 5 minutes. Money
is a game day (the real day's figure x 100, as the game counts it).

| Line | Riders a day | Mean trip | Fare a rider | Fares a day | Running | Upkeep | Profit a day |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| San Diego, light | 63,200 | 6.1 km | $3.71 | $23.5M | $7.2M | $1.1M | **+$15.1M** |
| Tijuana, light | 111,300 | 5.3 km | $0.94 | $10.5M | $7.6M | $1.2M | **+$1.7M** |
| Tijuana, busy | 119,800 | 5.2 km | $0.94 | $11.2M | $20.4M | $1.2M | **-$10.3M** |

- The Tijuana line carries 1.8x the riders and earns 0.45x the fares (fare a rider 0.25x: the scale's
  0.27x and slightly shorter trips), at the same costs. A player who sees 110,000 riders and crowded
  trains adds trains, and every train added costs what it costs in San Diego while earning a quarter.
  On the light service the trains probably run near their rated load in the morning peak (23 riders a
  car on average over the whole day).
- **Break-even load**: with a 4-car train ($3 a car-km) and a 5.5 km trip, a line pays its running
  cost when it averages 4.6 riders a car over the day in New York, 8.7 in Tokyo, 17 in Tijuana, 53 in
  Mumbai (rated load 160).
- **Which part of the gap is which.** The fare scale itself is right: Tijuana's default $0.65 is on
  the real-fare line, and Tijuana's real bus is $0.83. The whole difference in profit comes from costs
  not scaling. At the fare scale's 0.27x on running costs and upkeep, the busy line makes +$5.4M a game
  day and the light one +$8.1M. Every country's line would then pay at the same load per car.
- **Real costs do scale.** Farebox recovery shows no rise with income
  ([Wikipedia's table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farebox_recovery_ratio)). Fares cover: Istanbul
  Metro 90%, Hong Kong MTR 107%, Tokyo Metro 162%, Taipei 88%, Singapore SMRT 101%. On the low side:
  New York City Transit 30%, San Diego MTS 27%, Toronto 39%, LA Metro 11%. Cheap fares cover costs as
  often as dear ones, so real operating costs per rider fall with income about as fares do. Two rough
  figures from me: Delhi Metro's operating cost was ₹4,188 crore in 2024-25 (about $490M,
  [Storyboard18](https://storyboard18.com/brand-marketing/delhi-metro-posts-rs-412-8-crore-operating-surplus-in-fy25-as-revenues-rise-to-rs-4600-crore-89264.htm)),
  roughly $0.20-0.25 a journey. New York's subway is about $5-6 a trip. That is a power of about 0.9.
  London, Paris and Berlin spend about $6 a car-km, New York about $11.6
  ([Pedestrian Observations, 2026](https://pedestrianobservations.com/2026/05/22/quick-note-on-subway-operating-costs/)).
- **Construction does not scale the same way.** Building costs per km show no clear fall with income
  ([Transit Costs Project](https://transitcosts.com/)). The cheapest are in southern Europe, Korea and
  Turkey; India's and Latin America's tunnels sit in the middle; the US, UK and Singapore are dearest.
  Trains are bought on a world market. I would leave both at world prices.

## 4. What is generally optimal

Riders depend only on the fare as riders feel it (dollars x minutes a dollar). Today both use the
same 0.7, so **the default fare feels the same in every country**: the base is about 11 perceived
minutes in New York, Tokyo, Tijuana and Mumbai alike. One sweep per line (`run.py` at 0.1x to 8x the
default fares) gives the response:

| Line | Riders, default to 1.4x | Income-maximising fare | Default earns | Default carries |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tijuana, light | elasticity -0.56 | 2.8x the default ($1.80 base) | 78% of the best | 2.2x the riders |
| Tijuana, busy | -0.57 | 2.8x | 79% | 2.2x |
| San Diego, light | -0.67 | 2.0x ($5.00 base) | 86% | 1.7x |

- The best income sits at 2-3x the default fares in both countries, and the top is flat: 1.4x to
  2.8x earns within 10% of it on both lines. (T-1277 found the same on the real New York and London networks,
  about 4.5x its older, lower starting fares.) The model's fare elasticity at the default, -0.56 to
  -0.67, is near the real short-run figures of -0.3 to -0.4, a little stronger.
- **So the default is already a sensible, uniform setting**: about half the money-maximising fare,
  about 80% of its income, twice its riders. A player who touches nothing gets busy trains and decent
  money; one who wants cash can raise fares 1.5-2x and lose a quarter to two-fifths of the riders. The default
  does not need to move. If the value of time differs from the fare power, as in the options below,
  the default's place moves by country (at 0.6 the Tijuana default earns 70% of its best).
- The default fare is not the cause of the Tijuana loss. At the best fare, Tijuana's busy line earns
  $14.2M a game day against $21.6M of costs, so no fare setting makes it pay.

## 5. Options

Each option is a power on dollar income for the fare scale (fares), the minutes a dollar (value of
time) and running costs plus upkeep (costs; 0 = world prices as today), all anchored on the US (New
York is the same in every option). Tijuana is the measured test line. For Mumbai and Tokyo, "riders"
and "fare income" are the change on a busy line (Tijuana's sweep for Mumbai, San Diego's for Tokyo).
"Break-even" is the riders a car a line needs on average to cover a 4-car train's running cost (5.5 km
trips). A game day is 100x the real day.

What a player would see:
- **A (today)**: Mumbai's metro starts at about ₹17, Tokyo's at ¥195, close to real. Busy lines in poorer
  countries lose money however full they are, and a player sees no reason.
- **B and D (lower fare power)** move default fares off the real-fare line (Mumbai $0.43 against
  $0.23 real, Tokyo $1.58 against $1.20). They still leave the busy Tijuana line losing money, because
  costs are untouched. B also makes the default feel dearer the poorer the country (Mumbai riders -37%).
- **C (lower value of time only)**: poorer countries get more riders at the same fares, and a little
  more fare income with them, but the busy line still loses money.
- **E (costs follow fares)**: a line that pays in San Diego pays in Tijuana or Mumbai at the same load.
  Demand is untouched. Fares stay where the evidence puts them.
- **I** is E plus your "lower the value of time" at the step the evidence supports. Poorer countries
  get somewhat more riders at the default fares: Japan +5%, China and Mexico +8%, India +15%, Nigeria
  about +18%. Countries richer than the US (Singapore, Switzerland) get slightly fewer.
- **J** goes to 0.5, which only within-country studies of transit riders support. Tijuana's default
  then earns 62% of its best, so the default sits further below the money-optimal fare in poorer
  countries.

## Recommendation (Anita decides)

**Option I**: fares stay at income^0.7. Running costs and upkeep (rail and bus) are charged at each
country's default fare scale over the US's, so Mexico pays 0.27x, India 0.09x and Japan 0.53x. The
player's own fare multiple does not change costs. Construction and trains stay at world prices. The
value of time goes from 0.7 to 0.6 (4.4 x (GDP per head / $90,000)^-0.6: China and Mexico 13.5 minutes
a dollar, India 36, Japan 7.6). If you would rather not touch demand, **option E** (the cost half
alone) fixes the money on its own. The two halves are separate tasks: **T-2669** for costs, **T-2670**
for the value of time.

**Done when** (numbers to stop at):
- In a game started in San Diego, the busy Tijuana test line (`notes/T-2668/tij_line_big.json`: 16.6 km
  on a viaduct, 15 stations, 6-car trains every 2.5 minutes 6-20) shows a profit at default fares in
  its line panel: about +$6M a game day (today -$10M). The light one shows about +$9M (today +$1.7M).
- The break-even load for a 5.5 km trip is within 10% between Mumbai, Tijuana, Tokyo and New York
  (4.6 riders a car in all; today 53 / 17 / 8.7 / 4.6).
- New York unchanged: the real save's rail trips are within 0.1% of before, and its running costs and
  upkeep are the same to the dollar (the US factor is 1, and $90,027 is within 0.03% of the $90,000
  anchor).
- Ceiling tests A ≥ 85% and B ≥ 50% on the tree (London's weight goes from 6.0 to 5.8 minutes a dollar,
  so B moves up a little).
- Would a player see something obviously wrong? A full line in Tijuana or Mumbai pays, an empty one
  still loses, and the money panels and hovers say which country's prices a cost is at.

**What it changes in demand** (only T-2670; T-2669 touches money alone):
- At default fares, rail trips rise in every country poorer than the US, by about r^-0.1 in perceived
  fare (r = GDP per head over the US's): Japan's base fare 11.0 to 10.0 minutes, Mexico's 11.0 to 9.1,
  India's 11.0 to 7.7. On a busy line that is about +5% / +8% / +15% riders.
- The regional market weighs every country's fares at India's weight, which assumes the default fare
  feels the same everywhere (`fareFactors`, docs/demand-mode.md). With 0.6 against 0.7 that is no
  longer true, so each country's factor must carry r^(0.7 - 0.6) (or the market takes each origin's
  own weight).
- `docs/demand-worldwide.md` and `docs/demand-mode.md` ("the US 4.4, China 16, India 51") change with
  it. The car's money stays at 3 minutes a dollar everywhere: a separate oddity, not proposed here.

**Saves**: nothing stored changes in either half. An old save's costs and riders follow the new rules
from its next load, with no migration.

**Where it lands** (for the builder): the cost factor goes where running costs are summed (the clock
worker's `costParts`, `RUN_COST_*`) and where upkeep is (`TrackApi::upkeep`, `upkeepShares`,
`regionMoney.ts`), by the country of each edge's midpoint, as upkeep already splits by region. Train-km
on an edge take that edge's country, so a line from San Diego into Tijuana pays US prices north of the
border and Mexico's south of it. The Build tab's "$X / day upkeep", the station, track and line hovers,
and the Money tab's running cost would say the country's price level where it is not 1. In `income.ts`,
`FARE_E` splits into the fare scale's 0.7 and a new value-of-time power (0.6) for `fareWeight`.

## Method and limits

- The test lines run in otherwise empty networks (no feeders, no other lines), so riders reach them on
  foot or by car. Everything compares the same line at different fares or rules, and the shapes
  (income-maximising multiple, elasticity) matched T-1277's real New York and London networks.
- Riders were swept over fare multiples; options were read off the sweeps through perceived fare =
  dollars x minutes a dollar, which is how the sim uses them. For Mumbai and Tokyo the riders and income
  changes are the Tijuana and San Diego lines' response at those cities' perceived fares, not runs on
  their own packs.
- Running cost is the timetable's train-km (both ways, 14 hours at the day level and 4 at the evening
  level, none at night). Upkeep is the viaduct rate. Construction and loans are left out; construction
  is the same in every option (about $1.35B for the Tijuana line).
- Cores: one, claimed in `cores.py` for each sweep (each run took seconds).
