# T-2406: the network diagram at the large scale (design)

Anita, 2026-11-04: "also now that ive built a line from new york to california (lol) it further accentuates how
we're not willing to distort on the large scale. ideally we figure out a good way to do large scale distortion in
addition to local scale"

## Recommendation

**Move whole cities as blocks** (candidate 1, "cluster then connect"): each city's stations keep today's layout
and move together as one rigid block. A small solve places the blocks and the stations between them so that each
long hop gets about its target length along its real bearing. City boxes are pushed apart by a fixed gap. On the
mock, New York to Los Angeles goes from 41,612 pitches today (a rebuild) to 877, about 1.2 New York widths. The
cities' insides stay as they were: the median station moves 0.07 hops. This also does T-2168's block push and
island packing, so I'd build it as T-2168's first stage, about a day (section 4). The other two candidates, a
separate country-level view and a fisheye that follows the camera, are later options or not recommended.

## 1. What the layout does at large scale today

The code (`app/src/diagram/layout.ts`) already shortens long hops. What it lacks is anything that can move a city.
- **Targets are short.** `prep`: each hop's target is `L0 * (len / L0)^alpha` (alpha 0.3), and a lonely hop's is
  0.4 of that (`cityParts`, `LONELY_MIN` 4 pitches; it needs single-colour hops with stations of two hops or fewer,
  so a hop out of a hub such as Penn keeps the full evened length). The twelve hops of a New York to Los Angeles line
  (130 to 870 km each) want about 17 to 30 pitches each, **about 300 pitches in all**, under half of New York's
  width (720 pitches).
- **A holds the large-scale shape.** A starts at the real places (`geo`). Each round it holds every node to its last
  round's place (`wProx` 0.02) and to its real place (`wGeo` 0.001), against each hop's length term (`W_LEN` 0.6).
  If New York's 855 stations move together, the cost is 855 x 0.02^2 per unit squared. The chain of twelve hops
  pulls with 0.6^2 / 12. So a city moves about 7% of the way per round, and 10 rounds (`roundsA`; 40 only past
  8,000 complexes) leave about half the gap. T-2289 saw the same thing: "A does not converge on a country".
- **B holds A's result.** B uses `wProx` 0.1 and `wGeo` 0.01 to A's output, so a city as a whole does not move in B.
- **A rebuild never moves cities.** `warmStart` puts each known node at its last B place. B then runs 10 rounds held
  at `W_GEO_WARM` 0.05. Before the line was built, nothing linked New York and California, so A left each city
  at its real centre (T-2289's "Left": unlinked networks sit at their real distance). When the player then builds the
  line, the rebuild draws it at the true distance at the city scale. This is Anita's case.
- **Pieces get left behind.** Nothing knows which stations belong to a city, so when the line pulls a city, any
  piece of it with no track to the rest stays put: Staten Island's railway in New York, a detached Sendai line. The
  line's tension can also drag a hub station out of its city.

Measured on a mock graph (`artifacts/trainworld/t2406/`): New York's real network (`T-007-real-nyc.save`, 855
complexes) at New York, Sendai's (`data/packs/real/sdj.save`, trimmed to 35 km) moved to Chicago, and Toronto's
(`tor.save`, 455) moved to Los Angeles. These are stand-ins: no save has a Chicago or LA network, and Anita's own game
is in her browser. One fast line runs New York, Philadelphia, Harrisburg, Pittsburgh, Cleveland, Toledo, Chicago,
Omaha, Denver, Grand Junction, Salt Lake City, Las Vegas, Los Angeles. Each layout was run in Node by today's code.

## 2. How others do it

- **Wang and Chi, Focus+Context Metro Maps** (IEEE TVCG 2011),
  https://ir.lib.nycu.edu.tw/bitstream/11536/14562/1/000296241900086.pdf : an energy layout of the same family as our
  B, with a focus region magnified and the rest shrunk. It is done at layout time, because a fisheye warp applied
  afterwards bends octilinear lines.
- **Haunert and Sering, Drawing Road Networks with Focus Regions** (IEEE TVCG 2011, doi 10.1109/TVCG.2011.191): the
  focus enlarged by a convex quadratic program over edge distortions, with crossings forbidden. Much less distorted
  than a fixed fisheye projection.
- **Gansner, Koren and North, Topological Fisheye Views** (IEEE TVCG 2005), https://graphviz.org/documentation/GKN04a.pdf
  : coarser versions of the graph are made in advance and blended by distance from the focus. This is candidate 3's
  idea in its general form.
- **Böttger et al., Map Warping for the Annotation of Metro Maps** (2008),
  https://graphics.uni-konstanz.de/publikationen/Boettger2008MapWarpingAnnotation/index.html : the schematic warps the
  geographic map as you zoom.
- **Nöllenburg and Wolff's MIP** (TVCG 2011, https://i11www.iti.kit.edu/extra/publications/nw-dlhqm-10.pdf): edge
  lengths are a soft term, so long spans shrink only as far as the solver can manage. Hours per city. It has no notion
  of scale beyond that.
- **Multilevel layout** ("cluster then connect"): Walshaw's multilevel force-directed layout
  (https://jgaa.info/index.php/jgaa/article/view/paper70) and FM3 (Hachul and Jünger) collapse clusters, lay out the
  coarse graph, then refine. GMap (Gansner, Hu, Kobourov, https://arxiv.org/pdf/0907.2585) draws the clusters as
  regions. Keeping the boxes apart is rectangle overlap removal: Dwyer, Marriott and Stuckey, Fast Node Overlap
  Removal (GD 2005, https://doi.org/10.1007/11618058_15), as used in Graphviz and WebCola.
- **Multi-scale schematics**: Touya's keynote "Towards zoomable, multi-scale, interactive schematic maps?" (Schematic
  Mapping Workshop 2025, https://smw2025.sciencesconf.org/data/pages/schematic_mapping_workshop.pdf) covers breaks
  between scales and the disorientation they cause. That is candidate 2's risk.
- **UK National Rail map** (https://nre.co.uk/travel-information/maps-of-the-national-rail-network/): London and the
  South East are enlarged, with separate schematics for Birmingham, Leeds and Newcastle. Reviews fault its skewed
  Scotland and "strung-out" empty gaps (https://www.projectmapping.co.uk/Reviews/national_rail_atoc_maps.html). The
  older sheet put the enlarged areas on the back, which is candidate 2.
- **Amtrak**: the official map is geographic, with regional insets as far as I could find. Cameron Booth's subway-style Amtrak map
  (https://transitmap.net/amtrak) is topological: each long-distance route is a few straight runs between cities,
  sized by stops rather than miles, and the Northeast Corridor gets the room. Closest to candidate 1.
- **JR route maps** (JR Hokkaido, JR East; listed at https://www.mlit.go.jp/river/bousai/bousai-portal/en/tourism02/index.html):
  Tokyo or Sapporo enlarged, the empty lines of Hokkaido and Tohoku shrunk to even stop spacing. This is from looking
  at the maps; I found no source that states the rule.
- **Interrail and Eurail map** (https://WWW.SEAT61.COM/reference/interrail-and-eurail-map.pdf): cities as dots,
  routes as straight runs, Scandinavia and the east shrunk. No city interiors: a country level only, like
  candidate 2's zoomed-out view.

What they share: no national schematic keeps distance at the large scale. Cities keep their insides, and the gaps
between them are sized by what is in them (stops, lines), not by kilometres.

## 3. Candidates

**1. Cities as blocks** (recommended). Pictures: `c1-blocks.png`; `c1-blocks-cap8.png` with long hops capped at 8 pitches.
- *What a player sees*: every city drawn as today, in a faint box (T-2168), with the long lines between cities
  short. Each one leaves a city along its real bearing, so New York stays right of Chicago and Los Angeles sits to
  the lower left. Distances between cities follow the stops on a line, not the kilometres.
- *How it fits*: a stage between A and B. Blocks are cities by geography, not by track, so a detached piece rides
  with its city: `cityParts` cities, or better the game's zones (`cityWithin`), merged with stations within about
  15 km. The coarse graph is the blocks, the stations outside any block, and the hops between them. A small least
  squares solve gives each block a translation and each free station a place. Each hop gets its target (today's L,
  plus the distance from its station to its box edge) along its real bearing. Boxes keep a gap (12 pitches in the
  mock), and free stations are kept out of boxes. Then B as today. Islands and unlinked networks get a link to the
  nearest block, so they pack the same way (T-2168's Okinawa).
- *Stability*: a rebuild moves each block's last places, B and drawing alike, by the block's new translation.
  `warmStart` and the separation's pull to the last drawing then work as now. On the mock, adding the line moved the
  median city station 0.07 hops beyond its block's move (95th percentile 0.8). The blocks move only when the
  coarse graph changes, so most edits (inside a city) move nothing at this level.
- *Cost*: the coarse graph has tens to a few hundred nodes; 0.1 s unoptimised, a few ms as a proper sparse solve.
  It also takes A's job of pulling cities together, so a big network might not need A's 40 rounds (all of Japan
  is 80 s today); not measured.
- *Risks*: where cities blend into one another (the Tokaido belt, Boston to Washington), the block rule decides
  the whole shape. Such a corridor should stay one block, or the stage should apply only where a gap is longer than
  its target, leaving dense regions to today's A. A hub deep in a city makes the long hop cross the city's other
  lines, as it does today. The overall shape is the countries' shape bent toward the line's stop count: a
  coast-to-coast line with five stops draws shorter than a 300 km line with twenty.

**2. A country-level view** (inset style). Picture: `c2-country-level.png`, with the coarse graph laid out by
today's code: 45 x 7 pitches.
- *What a player sees*: zoomed out, each city is one small box with its name, and the long lines run between boxes,
  like the Interrail map. Zoomed in, a city's own diagram, with its long lines leaving by its edge ("Philadelphia,
  Los Angeles"), like the National Rail sheet's insets.
- *How it fits*: two layouts. The city layouts are today's, each laid out alone, so another city's edit never moves
  them. The country layout is today's code on the coarse graph. Stability is the best of the three.
- *Cost*: layouts are small and separate, but the view needs two levels, a switch or morph between them, clicks and
  hovers on both, and a picture of each. Two to three days.
- *Risks*: the jump between levels (Touya's disorientation). A line through three cities is cut into pieces. You
  can no longer see two cities' networks side by side.

**3. Fisheye that follows the camera** (focus+context). Picture: `c3-fisheye.png`: the same game looking at New
York, then at Los Angeles.
- *What a player sees*: the city in view at full size; other cities shifted toward it, more the further they are
  (distance d drawn as R ln(1 + d / R), R the city's size), each moved as a rigid block. Pan and they slide.
- *How it fits*: no layout change. It is done at drawing time, per frame, with the long pieces rerouted (two legs).
- *Cost*: cheap per frame (a few blocks, a few pieces). Names need placing again as the camera moves.
- *Risks*: the picture changes while you pan, so nothing has a fixed place. Far cities pile onto each other (in the
  Los Angeles view, Chicago lands on New York). The saved picture has no single answer. Wang and Chi do this at
  layout time for a reason. Not recommended.

## 4. The first stage

**T-2168a, block placement** (about a day; it becomes T-2168's first stage, and the faint boxes and names follow
as before):
- `cityBlocks(g)`: blocks from zones, or `cityParts` merged within about 15 km, with at least about 10 stations.
  The coarse graph is blocks, free stations, and the hops between blocks.
- `placeBlocks`: one small sparse least squares solve, with rectangle overlap removal with a gap, run between A and
  B on a fresh layout. On a rebuild, each block's last places are moved by its translation before `warmStart`.
