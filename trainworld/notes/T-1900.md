# T-1900 Slower trains blocking faster ones, and trains that should collide

notes/todo-2026-11-02.md. Asked before: T-334 open question 5 (2026-10-14), where she answered "an
express slowed by a local ahead is wanted but low priority"; that became T-339 (someday, "thats

## What the sim does today

**Shared track is a queue, not a line of trains.** Each piece of shared track, each platform track,
each junction and each flat crossing is a resource. From the timetabled trains an hour it gets a
utilisation and one average delay per train (Kingman's queueing formula, track-and-service.md 6.2,
`capacity.rs` `solve_one`). That delay is baked into every train of every line using it, as a hold
at the resource's entry (a slower approach, or a stop in front of it). The delay depends on how
busy the resource is and how many different lines share it (the "mix" term), not on how fast each
train is.

**So a slow train does not hold up a fast one.** An express behind a local on the same track gets
the same few seconds of queueing delay as the local, then runs at its own speed. Worked example from
the formulas (20 km, a stop every km, 80 km/h trains, 30 s dwell, 10 locals and 6 expresses an
hour each way on one double track):

| | free run | today's queueing holds | what it would be in real life |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local (all 21 stops) | ~32 min | about a minute | ~32 min |
| Express (ends only) | ~15.5 min | about a minute | ~30 min: it catches the local ahead within a few km and follows it |

So her "they should take the same speed": **no, the sim does not do this today.** The express
finishes in about half the local's time. Same for a high-speed train behind a normal train on
high-speed track (320 against 160 km/h): no blocking. Quad track only buys separate capacity; it
does not buy the express its speed, since the express has that already.

**Junctions and crowded interchanges.** A flat junction or flat crossing charges 90 s per move that
crosses another; the queueing delay from that is baked into each train's run. The hold is the same
for every train of the line on every trip, whether or not another train is actually there.

**What is drawn.** Every trip of a line, direction and demand level runs the same run profile from
its own departure time (keyframes on the GPU, architecture.md 6). Trains never look at each other:
two trains on one track, on one platform, or crossing at a flat junction are drawn through each
other. Each line's departure times are set from its own run alone (`Service::lattice`); nothing
lines them up against other lines on the same track, so two lines can leave a shared station
together and be drawn on top of each other for several stops. The numbers (run times, waits,
demand) and the drawing agree with each other; both just leave the blocking out.

## Options

### Numbers: does the express's run time rise?

**N1. A following loss on shared track (recommended).** For each pair of lines sharing a stretch in
one direction, a faster line behind a slower one loses time. From the two lines' free run times over
the shared stretch (difference `D`), the slower line's headway `H` and the minimum spacing `s` (the
section's occupancy, 1m47s at 80 km/h), the average loss of the faster line is `D - (H - s)/2`
when `D` is larger than `H - s` (it always catches up: it ends up at the local's speed less a
couple of minutes), else `D² / 2(H - s)` (some trips catch up, some do not). It resets where the
lines part or where the express is on its own track (quad, a separate route). The loss goes into
the faster line's run profile like any other hold, and so into round trips, trains needed, average
speed and demand.
- *Placed* as stops before the slower line's stops (the express halts outside each station the
  local is standing in, the loss split between them), not as one long stop at the start. That is
  what a held express looks like.
- *Effort*: one stage, in `capacity.rs` beside the section build, plus the line panel's round-trip
  split and the capacity hover ("held behind slower trains"). Settles in the existing damped solve.
- *Performance*: nothing measurable (one sum per pair of lines per shared stretch, on edits only).
- *Player sees*: an express on shared double track barely faster than its local; the line panel's
  average speed and the round-trip split say why. Quad track, a separate pair, or a crossover to
  overtake become worth building. High-speed trains on track shared with normal ones crawl.
- *Risk*: real networks built on shared pairs (T-1305: Tokyo's private railways, Paris RER) have
  passing tracks at stations in real life, which the game has no upgrade for. Their expresses
  would lose most of their speed. A passing-track station upgrade (T-334's option D, ruled out in
  T-1281 because overtaking cost nothing) becomes worth adding: a station with it resets the loss.

**N2. Event-driven block simulation** (T-265, "signals by clumps"). Every train simulated through
blocks; each trip gets its own keyframes; queueing comes out of the sim instead of a formula. The
true answer for both numbers and drawing. Weeks of work, per-trip storage, and demand still reads
averages from it. Not now.

### Drawing: bunching on screen

**D1. Space the departures (recommended, with N1).** Pick each line's departure offset per level so
trains of lines sharing track or a flat junction are spread out where they meet (real timetabling).
A greedy pass on edits: one number per line and level, saved as now. Fixes the "two trains leave
together drawn as one" case and most platform overlaps and junction collisions at stations close to
where lines meet. Cannot fix an express catching a local far down a long shared stretch (N1's
stop pattern then shows it trailing). Effort: one stage in `service.rs`. Performance: none at run
time. Waits are by headway, so demand does not change.

**D2. A display-only follow rule for trains in view.** For the trains on screen, any train that
would be drawn inside the one ahead on the same track is drawn a train length and a gap behind it,
then eases back to its timetable once clear; the same at a flat junction (a train waits drawn at the
signal while a crossing move is taken). Effort: one or two stages in the renderer (positions of
visible trains computed on the CPU each frame, an offset uploaded). Performance: small for hundreds
of trains in view. The lie: a held train is drawn behind where the numbers have it, so a platform
crowd may board a train that is still 20 s out; trains that leave the view can jump. Worth it only
after N1 and D1, for what is left.

**D3** is N2 above.

### Flat junctions

Numbers already charge them (90 s per crossing move, queueing as above). How doable is "trains wait
for each other": D1 spaces the lines so most crossings do not meet; D2 draws a wait at the junction
for trains in view; N2 is the full version. D1 then D2 gets most of the look at little cost.

## Recommendation

**N1 and D1 together** (T-1924, T-1925), then look at a busy interchange before deciding D2 (no
task yet; file one if overlaps still bother her). A passing-track station upgrade (T-1927) goes with N1, so expresses have somewhere to pass
on double track. Not N2.
