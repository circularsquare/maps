## T-1277: the money-optimal fare

### For Anita

- **Yes, the money-optimal fare is silly.** On the real New York and London networks revenue peaks
  at about 6x the starting fares: a $9 base plus $0.60 a km, about **$16.50 for the average rail
  trip**. That earns 2.9x what the starting fares earn while carrying half the riders. The curve is
  flat at the top: anything from 4x to 10x the starting fares earns at least 90% of the best.
- **Why**: a fare dollar counts 3 perceived minutes, and rail against the car goes on the ratio of
  their whole costs. At the starting fares the fare is a small part of rail's cost (the average
  $2.70 is 8 minutes beside 40-70 of walking, waiting and riding), so raising it loses few riders:
  +50% on every fare loses only 6.7% of New York's rail trips. Revenue keeps rising until the fare
  is about as big as the rest of the trip's cost.
- (Anita found a flat 9 too steep; round 2 below replaces this with a weight by national income.)
- **Round 1's proposal: make a fare dollar count 9 perceived minutes instead of 3**, for tickets only
  (rail and the plane), with the car's money left at 3. One constant. The money-optimal fare
  becomes **$3 base plus $0.20 a km, about $5.50 a trip**, near real fares. Riders then respond
  to fares about as much as real riders do: +50% loses 20% of trips. Ceiling A stays at 92%. B
  falls from 83% to 63% but still clears 50%.
- What it costs: at today's starting fares the real networks lose a quarter of their rail trips.
  To keep today's riders at the start, the starting fares would go to about $0.75 plus $0.05 a km.
  Fare income would then be half of today's, so running costs probably want a look in the same
  change.
- The other ideas did little. Making everyday trips alone more fare-sensitive only moves the
  optimum to 4-5x, because commuters keep paying. A steeper ratio curve moves it to 3-4x and
  pushes both ceilings up. A cap on the fare is simplest of all and leaves demand alone, but then
  the best play is always "fares at the cap".

### Why, from the rules

- **The fare is a small part of rail's cost.** Rail's cost is walks, wait, ride and the fare at 3
  perceived minutes a dollar ($20 an hour, T-067, "chosen for play"). Rail's share of rail + car
  is 1 / (1 + q^3), q = rail / car, so a 1% dearer rail trip loses 3(1 - s)% of its share, and a
  1% dearer fare only that times the fare's part of rail's cost. Measured arc elasticity of rail
  trips to the fare at x1: -0.13 in New York (real transit: about -0.3 to -0.4 in the short run).
  Revenue only stops rising where that reaches -1, which is where the fare's minutes are about
  as large as the rest of rail's cost: fares of $10-20 for trips whose walks, waits and rides
  come to 30-60 minutes.
- **The car is dear where rail runs.** The car pays its drive at 1.33, 7 minutes to park, paid
  parking by job density (up to 50 minutes), home parking in dense places, and ownership at $2 a
  trip plus $0.13 a km. In Manhattan or central London it often costs twice rail's minutes, so
  rail can charge a lot before it loses the ratio.
- **The 3-4x fade never binds.** At the optimum rail costs about 1.2-1.5 times the car on the
  trips it keeps; the fade starts at 3x.
- **The 100x days do not move the optimum.** Fares and running costs both scale by 100, and with
  the service fixed the running cost does not depend on the fare, so the money-optimal fare is the
  revenue-optimal one. (Crowding falls a little as riders leave; it is in the runs.)
- **The ratio rule has no scale.** Doubling every cost of a trip leaves its shares alone, so the
  optimum is a fixed part of each trip's whole cost: long commuter trips bear proportionally
  dearer fares, which the per-km rate already gives them. Scaling the curve keeps that shape.

- **Weighting the fare is exact rescaling.** With fares weighing w, riders at a fare F are
  today's riders at w x F, so the optimum moves to 1/w of today's in dollars and carries the same
  riders (about half of today's at the starting fares). It changes how many dollars the best fare
  is, not the trade between riders and money. At 3x, elasticity at the starting fares becomes
  -0.40 in New York (8.82M to 7.05M trips for +50%), in the range real transit shows.
- **Per-purpose weights** do little on their own: commutes, which keep the weight 1, set the
  optimum, and it would add a factor per purpose. Not recommended.
- **A steeper ratio curve** changes all of mode choice to move the optimum a little. Not
  recommended for this.
- **A cap** (say a base of at most $3 and $0.30 a km) needs no demand change and leaves the
  ceilings alone, but the money-optimal fare is then always the cap (at x2 the real networks earn
  60% of today's peak), so the choice between riders and money disappears into a ceiling.

**Recommendation: fares weigh 3x, as one constant for tickets (rail fares in every layer, and
the plane's fare in the regional market, so rail against the plane keeps its balance); the
car's money (ownership, running, crossings) stays at 3 minutes a dollar.** Two follow-ups decided
with it: the starting fares (keep $1.50 + $0.10 and accept 25% fewer rail trips at the start, or
$0.75 + $0.05 for 93% of today's riders), and running costs, since fare income at the riders-heavy
end would roughly halve. Both the plane change and the starting fares need the regional market and
the trip explorer's mirror (`marketRule.ts`) to follow. Not built: Anita's call (a demand factor).

## Round 2: the fare's weight by national income (with T-1166)

Anita (2026-10-25): 9 minutes a dollar everywhere is too steep; base it on national income, about
9 in poorer countries. Measured and proposed here, not built.

### For Anita

- **Yes, the data supports it, and we already hold the income figures.** Across studies the value
  of time rises with income at an elasticity of about 0.5-1: 0.9 in the UK meta-analysis, 0.7 in
  the EU's HEATCO guidance, 0.5-1 in European countries' own appraisal rules, and about 1 where
  value of time is tied to wages (the usual World Bank and US DOT practice). GDP per head at PPP
  for 240 countries (World Bank 2025) is already in the world field the market uses.
- **The rule I'd use**: minutes per fare dollar = 5 x (country GDP per head / $60,000)^-0.7,
  at PPP. That gives the US 4.2, Germany 4.8, the UK and France 5.4, Japan 5.8, Turkey 7.0, China
  9.2, Mexico 10, Brazil 11, Indonesia 13, India 17.5, Nigeria 20 and Ethiopia 40.
- **What it does to the money-optimal fare** (a fare dollar now counts 3 minutes everywhere):

| City | Minutes a $ | Best fare, average trip | Best fare as a multiple of today's starting fares | Ceiling A | Ceiling B |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| New York | 4.2 | about $11.70 (real network) | about 4x | 92.2 | 78.6 |
| London | 5.4 | about $9.20 (real network) | about 3.3x | 92.3 | 75.5 |
| São Paulo | 10.8 | $3.56 (ceiling network) | 1.5x | 96.2 | 70.0 |
| Mumbai | 17.5 | $2.30 (ceiling network) | 1x | 96.3 | 66.2 |
| Lagos | 20.2 | $1.49 (ceiling network) | 0.67x | 95.4 | **46.9** |

- So rich cities keep a best fare of about 3-4x the starting fares ($9-12 a trip), which is the
  price of keeping them at 4-6 minutes. Poorer cities land near or below today's starting fares.
- **The catch is the single fare curve.** The game has one fare curve for the whole network. In
  US dollars everywhere, one curve cannot suit New York and Lagos at once, and Lagos fails ceiling
  B (47%) because B's $1.50 fare is dear there.
- **So yes, I'd charge fares in local terms too:** the player sets one curve (as it is today),
  and each trip pays it times its country's income factor (GDP per head over $60,000, to the
  same 0.7 power). Then the income term cancels for demand: everyone weighs the curve at 5
  minutes a curve-dollar, every city has the same best curve (about 3.6x the starting curve), and
  ceiling B is 76-90% everywhere. Poorer cities just bring in less money per rider (Lagos pays
  about a fifth of New York's fare for the same ride). With that, the starting curve can stay at $1.50
  + $0.10 a km. New York keeps 91% of today's rail trips at it.
- The plane's fare: at the trip's origin weight, in US dollars (airfares are a world price). The
  car's money: left at 3 minutes a dollar for now. Scaling it too would be consistent but would
  move every poorer city's mode split, so it wants its own measured step.

### The evidence

- **Wardman, Chintakayala and de Jong (2016), "Values of travel time in Europe"**, Transportation
  Research A 94: 3,109 values from 389 European studies. Values vary with GDP, distance, purpose
  and mode, and transfer across countries. I could not read its exact GDP elasticity.
- **Abrantes and Wardman (2011), the UK meta-analysis**: elasticity of value of time to GDP per
  head 0.9, highly significant.
- **HEATCO (EU guidance)** recommends 0.7 over time, from the meta-studies of Wardman and of
  Shires and de Jong. **Shires and de Jong (2009)**, an international meta-analysis built values
  for 25 EU states from GDP.
- **The UK DfT scoping study on values of time** (2018): elasticities in use in most European
  countries lie between 0.5 and 1. Swedish work does not reject 1 over time. Binsuwadan, Wardman,
  de Jong and others (2023): meta-analyses give the largest income effects, repeated surveys the
  smallest.
- **The World Bank's and US DOT's practice** ties value of time to wage rates (US DOT: personal
  local travel at half the wage), which is an elasticity of 1 across incomes.
- 0.7 sits in the middle. At 0.5 the spread is gentler (US 4.4, UK 5.3, China 7.7, Brazil 8.7,
  India 12.2, Nigeria 13.6), and Lagos then passes B at 62.6% with fares in US dollars. At 1.0 it
  is steep (India 30, Ethiopia 97).

**Data and the PPP choice.** `data/work/world_field.countries.json` holds `gdp`, GDP per head at
PPP in constant 2021 international dollars (World Bank NY.GDP.PCAP.PP.KD, 2025), for 240
countries. The market rows carry it as `inc`, capped at the US, so the rule would need the
uncapped figure: Switzerland, Norway and Singapore come out below the US, at 3.6-3.9. I'd use PPP
for three reasons:
- The game's dollar is one world price list (track, trains and fares cost the same everywhere),
  so it behaves like an international dollar.
- PPP is the figure the market already uses.
- Nominal income would spread poorer countries two to four times further (India's nominal GDP
  per head is about a quarter of its PPP figure), giving 40-60 minutes a dollar there.

**How it would reach the sim:** each zone pack carries its country, and `api.rs` already has a
per-country table hook (`COUNTRY_ASC_RAIL`). A per-country minutes-a-dollar, read from the
registry, would replace `FARE_MIN_PER_USD` for fares in the zone's own layers, and the origin
zone's value would be used in the regional layer. Fares in local terms would multiply the fare
income in `money.ts` by the boarding zone's income factor, and the trip explorer and the fare
hovers would show the local fare.

## T-1275: could a transatlantic line pay?

### For Anita

- **No, and it should not.** A New York - London high-speed line loses money on running costs
  alone, before upkeep, at any fare and any frequency, and its construction is about $1.5
  trillion, 250 times the starting cash.
- The plane is what limits it: rail is 18.7 hours against about 8 hours in the air, so the fare that
  earns most is about $130-155, roughly $0.027 a km. A full 20-car high-speed train costs about
  $0.029 a passenger-km to run at 70% of crush, so even full trains do not cover the running cost.
- **A hole worth fixing (a task for the orchestrator to file):** open ocean outside every city
  pack's water mask counts as land, so track can be laid at ground level across the Atlantic and
  priced as land: about $0.38 trillion instead of $1.51 trillion, with no bridge. A coarse world
  water mask for pricing and the level-0 rule would close it.

### The numbers

- **Market** (`notes/T-1277/atlantic.py`, the market rule from `registry.json` and
  `market-rows.json`): New York and London are 5,562 km apart; their places trade 1,965 trips each
  way a weekday (the real air market is about twice that). Places within 200 km of each: 3,875;
  within 400 km, as a feeder network might reach: 14,459. All the US and Canada with all of
  Europe: 134,435. The average day is 1.167 weekdays.
- **The plane**: 1,788 perceived minutes door to door (88 minutes of airport drives, 90 at the

### What would have to change, and whether it should

To pay at all, three things at once: fares that cover the running cost (a plane far dearer or
slower in the rule, or high-speed running costs about half), several times the trips (the model's
New York - London market is already the busiest long-haul pair), and construction two orders of
magnitude cheaper to pay back the build in any sane playtime (at a net of $1M a real day, $1.51T
takes 15,000 game days, about 380 hours at the top speed). The real world agrees: no fixed link has
been seriously costed under trillions. None of these is worth changing for this; the plane and
the ocean are the game's way of saying "not here". The only change I would make is the unmasked
ocean, so the price at least reflects the sea.
