from all tagged hospitals (162) instead of tagged points (45): New York 19.7% but the others 2
points worse; typical levels 2 to 5 (no effect, 30-32% on the linear rule); the bed range clipped

**Result: 20.1%, a tenth of a point over the target.** Every variant sits at 20-22% on New York
and 26-28% on average elsewhere, against 22% and 30% for one size for all: floor area tells big
hospitals from small only weakly in OSM (campus polygons hold offices and car parks, buildings
are mapped in part, tags sometimes give a whole system's beds). The rule is still well ahead of
what it replaces (31%), and in the packs that need it, it mostly decides small point hospitals
against large campuses, where it is right in direction. I kept the round exponent rather than
pick 0.4 for its 19.9%, which would be fitting the held-out pack.

## The national scale and the cap

The scale is the World Bank's beds per 1,000 x the pack's population over the rule's raw total.
Across all 67 packs (survey with the shipped table): scales 0.26 (Lagos) to 4.3 (`szo`); the
largest site Osaka's one tagged hospital, 1,063 x 2.81 = 2,987 beds; then Shanghai 2,296, Berlin
2,240, Mumbai 1,443. Largest share of a pack over 5%: the small US packs (Windham's one hospital
100%, Litchfield 57%, New Haven 47%, New London 44%, Springfield 28%) and Berlin 5.4%.

The cap: a site keeps at most 5% of the pack's beds or 1,500, whichever is more, and never more
than 4,000; the excess goes to the others in proportion. 5% is judgement with room to spare: in
packs over 10M people the largest site holds 0.3-3.4% (New York 1.8%, Mumbai 3.2%); the 1,500 floor lets a small pack keep its one regional hospital; the
4,000 ceiling keeps the gate's "largest hospital under 5,000" safe where a high national rate
meets few mapped sites (Japan and Korea at 12.6-12.8 a 1,000). On today's packs it binds only on
Berlin's largest site (2,240 to 2,091).
