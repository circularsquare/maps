## Design (T-295 design stage, 2026-10-14)

### Her question: how does the game compare driving with the train?

Each trip adds up both options in "perceived minutes" and splits riders by the gap. Rail = access
walk (2.0 a minute for the first 10, 8.0 after) + half the headway (capped at 5) x 1.37 + ride +
5 per transfer + egress walk + fare at 3 min a dollar. Car = drive minutes x 1.33 + 3 to park +
parking by job density at the destination (1 a minute per 2,500 jobs/km², capped at 40). The
share is a logistic: **every 10 perceived minutes the train saves multiplies its odds by 1.65**
(0.05 a minute); equal cost is 50/50, 20 minutes better is 73%, 40 better 88%. The per-city
propensity (New York 0) shifts that line for car ownership and habit: +1 is worth 20 minutes.

Worked (stations 1 km from home and work, a train every 10 minutes, free fare, scratchpad
`t295/examples.py`). The rail side starts 28 minutes behind (21 walking, 7 waiting):
- 20 km suburb to suburb: the car costs 40 (27 real minutes). The train must ride in 12 minutes
  (100 km/h door average) just to tie; at 60 km/h it gets 36%.
- 20 km suburb to a mid-sized downtown: the car costs 74, mostly the 11-minute parking and slow
  city streets. A 26 km/h train ties; at 60 km/h rail gets 76%.
- 60 km suburb to suburb: the car costs 103. A 48 km/h train ties; 100 km/h gets 85%.
- 60 km into a downtown: rail wins almost every trip, because the model drives the whole middle
  of a trip at the mean of both ends' speeds (136 minutes for 60 km into a 30,000/km² place;
  real is about 70). That is a bug for this task (stage 1 below).

**So:** what matters is the gap, not the train's speed alone. Parking at the destination is the
biggest single factor (up to 43 minutes), then the walks and waits at both ends (about 28 together),
then speed, transfers, fare and the propensity. Trip length matters because the fixed 28 minutes
are paid once, so a train beats the car more easily the longer the trip.

### Decisions for Anita

1. **Keep the fast walk and add a "ride" (car, bus, bike, lift: all one) as a second way to reach
   a station** (my pick), or replace the fast walk with a true 5 km/h walk plus the ride (truer,
   but New York's calibration starts again).
2. **Which trips get the ride**: commutes (local and between zones) and long trips only (my
   pick: park-and-ride is mostly commuting, and it keeps the solve cheap); everyday outings keep
   walking only. Or every purpose.
3. **Ride at the far end**: long trips yes (taxi or pick-up, no parking), commutes no (the car is
   at home; the evening trip is the morning's reversed). My pick.
4. **The 30% is an output, not a setting** (my pick). The surveys below say a lone suburban
   commuter station keeps 85-90% of its riders without feeders, while a central station in a
   transit city keeps far less. Forcing one number everywhere would be wrong in both places.
5. **Car parks are automatic and unlimited**, priced by job density at the station (my pick for
   now). A buildable car park with a capacity is a possible later gameplay task.

### The model

**Access cost per station** = a logit (the existing station logit, scale 0.2) over two entries:
- walk: today's fast walk, unchanged (2.0, then 8.0 after 10 minutes);
- ride: `P_ride + 1.33 x drive_min(home, station) + station_end`, with the drive minutes from the
  existing density model and routed factor, `station_end` = min(parking at the station by its
  job density, the same formula as a destination's; `D_drop`, a drop-off), `P_ride` about 10 and
  `D_drop` about 10 to start (fitted, below).

That is her curve: in a suburb the combined cost is 9 perceived minutes at 1 km, 17 at 2 km, 22
at 3 km, 25 at 5, 33 at 10, 49 at 20 km. Linear (10 a km) to about 2 km, then about 1.6 a km. The
8.0 tail stays for anyone the ride does not suit, but past 2 km the ride is cheaper anyway. Near a
station the extra option lowers access by about a minute, so walk-only calibration barely moves.

**Which stations a ride considers** (a ride reaches dozens): within `ride_max_m` straight line
(15 km for commutes, 40 km for long trips), keep the cheapest station **on each line**, at most
`K_ride` = 6 lines by ride cost. Real park-and-riders skip the local stop for the line they want,
and nearest-per-line keeps that choice without testing every station. Grouped per 2 km zone
(the ride cost barely changes inside one), not per cell.

**Whole trip**: the rail alternative is then priced as today (logsum over access x strategy x
egress) and compared with driving the whole way, which is exactly her "drive to the station,
pay the parking penalty, take the train, or drive the whole way".

### Why T-034's drive leg doubled suburban rail, and what is different

T-034 (2026-10-09) priced the leg as 30 + 2.5x drive minutes with no cost at the station end, on a
gravity sending New Jersey to Manhattan at 2-4x the real rate and roads at a flat 28 km/h (Suffolk
75 minutes by car against ACS's 29). It carried 25% of rail trips and put Long Island at 17% rail
(ACS 8.4%). Since then: the gravity has T-162/T-198's border factor and curve, roads have the
density speeds (T-090) and routes (T-275). Still to guard against:
- **parking at the station end** by density, so driving to a Midtown station costs what parking
  in Midtown costs; a Long Island car park costs 3;
- **long drives into dense places too slow** (above; also suburban car commutes 36-47 minutes
  against ACS 27-33): rail would take nearly every long commute into a city once a ride reaches it.
  Stage 1 fixes this first;
- **the fast walk is already bike-like** (15 km/h, 2-3 km): the ride starts where it fades, not on
  top of it. If the suburbs overshoot, raise `P_ride`, not the walk.

### Both layers, and T-362

- **Local layer**: commutes, home end only (decision 3).
- **Regional layer**: market sites get ride entries next to their walk entries (today up to eight
  station options per zone end); commutes between zones at the home end only, long trips at both
  ends, the far end as a drop-off (`D_drop`, no parking). Long trips choose at 0.025 a minute, so
  a ride of 30-40 minutes to a good intercity station is normal, as it is in life.
- **Assumed interface with T-362** (designed in parallel): it produces trips between places (sites
  or zone centres) with its own whole-trip car, and prices rail from per-end station options.
  T-295 supplies one shared function, `access_options(place, end, purpose) -> [(station,
  perceived_min, mode)]`, used by the zone packs, the market and T-362 alike. A zone centre is
  enough for ride entries; walk entries need sites.

### The fitting statistic

The real number to fit is the share of rail riders who reach the station by car (driving, being
dropped off, carpooling), since in the game the fast walk already covers bikes and short buses:
- GO Transit (Toronto, 2018): drive alone and park 62%, dropped off 15%, carpool 4.5%, transit
  8.5%, walk 8.5%, bike 1% (Globe and Mail, from Metrolinx; Metrolinx counts 55% non-drive-and-park
  "in 2019" by a wider definition).
- VRE (Virginia, 2016-2019 surveys): drove alone and parked 82-84%, dropped off 6-7%, walk ~5%.
- Metro-North (MTA 2014 to NYMTC): inbound riders mostly drive, 51% driving alone on trips to
  Manhattan; over half of outbound (reverse) riders arrive by transit.
- UK National Rail (Transport Focus 2011, NTS): most walk, car (parked or dropped off) second; no
  exact figure found. Amtrak (1972, dated): car 62%, taxi 17%.

**Targets on New York's real save**: ride share of home-end access 50-70% at Long Island,
Westchester/Connecticut and outer New Jersey stations; rail share by county group still within
about 20% of ACS (`pipeline/realnet_groups.py`); on the corridor test network
(`research/intercity/corridor_net.py`) commute legs between zones rising from a third to a fifth
of real towards 0.7-1.3x. **The 30% check**: the same network with one station per city against
one with a feeder line, per city. From the surveys a lone suburban station should keep about
85-90% (only the 8-15% who come by transit are at stake, and some of them would drive instead);
the 30% is plausible only for a central station in a transit city.

### Worldwide

The ride is any non-walking way to the station, so nobody is shut out of it for lack of a car,
and nothing new is per-country: drive speeds come from density, parking from job density, both
global. In Mumbai the ride is a bus, auto-rickshaw or share-auto through slow dense streets (the
density model gives 15-20 km/h) with no parking, so `station_end` is the drop-off; the whole-trip
car is weak through the propensity, and walking and feeders dominate there by themselves. What may
not carry over: `P_ride` is fitted on North American car parks; where the ride is a bus, the
fixed cost includes the wait and should be larger. Check: a ride-share figure per sampler city
(UK NTS for London). demand-worldwide.md gets an entry in the build stage.

### Cost in the kernel

The logsum already separates access from the pair loop: extra access entries cost entries x S
per origin subzone, which is small. The cost is new origins: zones beyond the walk that a ride now
reaches become origin rows of the pair loop. Bounds: one subzone per ride-only zone (no walk
bands); `pair_walk_max` (80) applies to ride access plus egress walk, which keeps destinations near
stations; home end only, so destination rows do not grow; everyday purposes untouched. Budget: the
real New York day (27 s natively) at most +25%; if over, ride-only origins group into 6 km
squares as the market's do.

### Stages (TASKS.md T-370 to T-374, in this order)

1. Long drives into dense places (prerequisite): the middle of a drive runs at the open-road
   speed, the dense end's slowness only on its last few km; recheck ACS car times. Small.
2. Ride access in the local commute: the ride entry, nearest station per line, ride-only
   subzones, home end only, `P_ride` and `D_drop`, counters for ride share; measure solve time.
   Medium (3-4 agent hours).
3. Fit and checks: New York real save, corridor network, the 30% check, London. Medium.
4. Ride access in the regional layer and long trips, both ends for long trips, the shared
   `access_options` T-362 uses. Medium.
5. Show it: riders arriving by ride in the station inspector and its hover; docs and
   demand-worldwide.md. Small.

### Risks

Long commutes into dense centres go almost all rail until stage 1 lands; suburban rail can
overshoot as in T-034 (the fit watches county shares first); nearest-per-line misses an express
stop when the line's local stop is nearer; unlimited car parks let a lone station in a big suburb
draw more than real car parks hold.
