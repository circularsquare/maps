# T-3029: a price-strict rider group (proposal, no code)

Anita, 2026-11-10: some riders could care about price very strictly ("completely broke"), and would
take a long-distance bus when it is cheaper than owning a car.

## Groups today

- **Local layers** (commutes, everyday trips, the trip explorer): one group. A fare dollar costs
  4.4 x (GDP per head / $90,000)^-0.5 perceived minutes by the trip's country (US 4.4, India 25).
  The car's money is 3 minutes a dollar everywhere. Rail and the car split by the curve
  1 / (1 + q^3) on the cost ratio (docs/demand-mode.md 4.3).
- **Regional market** (trips of 25 km and more between zones, T-1431, docs/regional.md 5.1): each
  country's people are three equal income groups, from World Bank quintile shares. Every group weighs
  every mode's money (car running cost, plane fare, rail fare, bus fare) at the country's
  minutes a dollar times (group income / mean)^-0.5 times 0.4. The poorest third (income 0.35-0.49 of
  the mean) weighs a dollar 1.4-1.7 times the average, the richest third 0.7-0.8 times. A group makes
  longer trips in proportion to (income / mean)^0.7. Choice is a damped logit among rail (bus lines
  count as network lines here), car and plane, worked out per group and added.
- There is no car ownership in demand (T-854). The car costs its running cost a road km (gasoline and
  distance tolls, split over two occupants, about 7 cents a km each in the US) plus parking.

So "money against time" groups exist, but only on the regional market, and the poorest third
already is the price-sensitive one.

## Would a broke group change long-distance bus use

Probably little, though I have not run it. In the regional logit the poorest third (weight 1.6)
already favours a bus that undercuts the car's running cost. A fourth group standing for, say, the
poorest 10% (income around 0.15 of the mean, a guess) would weigh money about 2.5 times the average
and, by the 0.7 power, make about a quarter as many long trips as the mean. So it is a group that
is price-strict and rarely travels: the share of long-distance trips it would add is a few percent
of a country's total. A bus line the poorest third already ignores because it costs more than the
car's fuel per person would be ignored by them too. The fare multiple (T-3028) already lets the
player price a bus at the car's running cost, which is what this group would react to; from the
profit runs in T-3028, a bus at 0.25x loses money anyway, so the group's effect would show mostly as
riders on a line that already loses money.

## Cost to add

- **Sim**: a fourth group in the regional market multiplies its group loop by 4/3 (the mode choice
  is per group per zone pair; the local layers never have groups, notes/T-1431.md: adding them there
  would cost about 2.4 times the solve time). Roughly a third more regional solve time.
- **Data per country**: the quintile table gives the lowest 20% share; "broke" would need a
  poverty headcount (World Bank PIP, $3/day line) per country, a second global source to join and
  keep, with its own gaps (survey years, 170 countries). Or a rule on the existing table (split the
  bottom third in two), which costs no data.
- **Demand rules touched** (docs/demand-worldwide.md): "Income groups on longer trips" (the three
  equal groups and the 0.7 trip-rate exponent), "Value of time and fare sensitivity" (the minutes a
  dollar), and the ceiling tests (A at least 85%, B at least 50% on rail plus car), which a changed group
  split could move. "Demand is simple on purpose" asks Anita to review any added factor first.

## Recommendation

Don't add a group now. The poorest third already weighs price 1.4-1.7 times the average, a "broke"
group would barely travel (trip rate falls with income^0.7), and the bus fare multiple gives the
player the price lever directly. If Anita still wants the effect visible, the cheap version needs no
new data: split the poorest third into two groups by the same quintile table (the bottom and middle part of
the poorest third), at a cost of one more pass over the regional group loop, and measure the long-distance bus
share against it before keeping it.
