# T-3029: a price-strict rider group (stage 2: measured)

Anita, 2026-11-10: riders who care only about price, e.g. broke ones who would take a long bus when
it beats the car. Stage 2: does a fourth group cost performance, and what does splitting the poorest
third look like.

## Groups today

- Local layers (commutes, everyday trips): one group.
- Regional market (trips of 25 km and more): three equal income groups per country from the World
  Bank quintile table (`app/src/game/income.ts`, `incomeGroups`). A group weighs every mode's money
  (car running cost, plane fare, rail and bus fare) at the country's minutes a dollar times
  0.4 x (group income / mean)^-0.5, and makes long trips in proportion to (income / mean)^0.7.
  Bus lines are lines in the same network, so they are priced the same way.

## The trial (not on by default)

The trial is not in the code: it is `notes/T-3029-split.patch` (`git apply` it from trainworld/, then
`npm run wasm --prefix app`), kept out so `GROUPS` 4 costs nothing while unused (orchestrator, 2026-11-10).
The poorest third becomes the poorest sixth and the next sixth, from the same table. Switch:
`VITE_INCOME_SPLIT=1` in the environment of the Vite that serves the page (`INCOME_SPLIT` in
`income.ts`). The Rust side only needed `GROUPS` raised from 3 to 4 (`sim/src/demand/intercity.rs`);
no WASM interface change, and a place with three groups behaves as before. Both cargo suites pass
(436 and 616 tests). Cost of the constant: 8 bytes more per market place.

What the split does to the numbers (the factor is the money weight on top of the country's):

| | poorest third | poorest sixth | next sixth | middle third | top third |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| US, 3 groups: factor / share of long trips | 0.67 / 17% | | | 0.45 / 29% | 0.29 / 54% |
| US, split: factor / share of long trips | | 0.79 / 7% | 0.60 / 10% | 0.45 / 29% | 0.29 / 54% |
| India, 3 groups | 0.50 / 24% | | | 0.42 / 32% | 0.33 / 44% |
| India, split | | 0.54 / 11% | 0.48 / 13% | 0.42 / 32% | 0.33 / 44% |

The poorest sixth weighs money only 18% more than the poorest third did in the US (8% in India),
because weights follow income^-0.5. It also makes fewer long trips (7% of them). So the split moves
little by itself.

## Performance

- Real save, page, 3 demand workers, same machine, one run each: demand final in 97 s (3 groups) and
  74 s (split); that gap is machine load from other agents, not the split. Worker memory 378 MB
  either way. The solve in the real save is almost all local layers.
- Rust solve of the test market (300 solves): 3.82 ms with one group, 4.09 ms with three, 4.10 ms
  with four. A group costs about 3.5% of the regional solve, so a fourth is about 3% more, not a third
  more. The solve is mostly work done once per zone pair, with a short loop per group inside it.
  (A small fixture; a world-sized market has more pairs but the same per-group loop.)
- No new data and no new files: the split uses the quintile table already there.

## What changes for a player

- The real save, a 159 km express bus from Penn Station to Philadelphia at 1 bus an hour: 2,853 bus
  riders at 1x and 3,397 at 0.5x, identical with and without the split. Rail riders over all lines:
  19,051,743 against 19,051,744. Total trips 907,474,309 both ways. Rail share of the busiest
  corridor in the test market: 0.9753 against 0.9752.
- Even with the poorest sixth's factor pushed to 2.0 (three times the US value) the test pair's rail
  share stays 0.9753. The long-trip share of that sixth is only 7%, so a big weight on a small group
  barely moves the totals.
- For a player the gain is nothing visible; the lie removed is that all the poor travel alike.

## Recommendation

The split costs little (about 3% of the regional solve, no memory worth naming) and can stay behind
the switch, but it does nothing a player would see, so don't turn it on. If a "completely broke"
group should visibly love a cheap long bus, the group's size and weight must change, not just its
count: a poorest tenth with its own money weight (say 3x the average, from a poverty line, not the
quintile table) that still makes some long trips. That needs a poverty headcount per country (World
Bank PIP) and a rule for why such a group travels at all (the 0.7 power gives it a quarter of the
average trip rate). Worth doing only if Anita wants buses to matter to the very poor; it needs her
call on the weight, since no source gives it.
