# T-1090: do balanced areas walk to work too much? (brainstorm, 2026-10-23)

No rule changed. Measurements on the shipped packs, read-only; scripts in the session scratchpad
(listed under "Method"). Proposals filed as T-1091 to T-1093.

## For Anita

**1. Has it been tested?** Yes in parts, and you are right outside the US. New York was checked
against LODES at block level and matches it: 3.2% of commutes stay in their home 2 km square,
against LODES's 3.5%. A balanced suburb gets 3.2% in both, and commutes walk 5.1% against the
census's 6.2%. Everywhere else the near end of the curve was never checked at 2 km, and commutes
walk two to three times the census. London's commutes walk 16.6% against the 2011 census's 9.4%,
and 47% of them are under 5 km against the census's 38%. Before T-636, Tokyo's walked 22% against
7.5%, Sendai's 26% against 8% and Paris's 13% against 7%. The fill-up is not the cause. Solving the
same packs with no fill-up (every worker shared over jobs by distance alone) keeps *more* commutes
in the home square, not fewer: London 16.4% against 13.9%, Tokyo 22.8% against 19.3%. The cause is
the curve's steep near end. Errands and leisure trips have no fill-up at all: each home shares its
outings over destinations by distance alone.

**2. Is there a dial?** Several (table below). The one that hits "balanced areas walk too much"
squarely is the commute curve's near end: how much a job in your own 2 km square counts against
one in the next square. Outside the US the curves are steep (d^-1.4 to d^-2.2). They were fitted on
borough or municipality pairs, or on distance bands with everything under 4 km lumped together,
so nothing fitted the first 2 km. They were extended inward from there. 83% of the world's commutes
run on two borrowed curves fitted that way. The fix is to hold the curve flat inside 2 km, so your
own square counts like a neighbouring one. Own-square commutes then drop by about 60% (London 13.9%
to 5.4%, Tokyo 19.3% to 6.2%, Lagos 15.7% to 5.9%). The mean commute grows only 0.4 to 0.8 km, and
commute walking falls by roughly 3 to 7 points.

**3. What do models usually do?** Commutes are doubly constrained, as ours are, or use destination
choice with "shadow prices" that fill each zone's jobs, which comes to the same thing. Shopping and
leisure are singly constrained, as ours are, usually with a different "size term" for each purpose
(retail jobs for shopping, a separate mall term, and so on). The near end is set by calibrating
against the observed trip-length distribution, intrazonal trips included. That calibration is the
step we skipped at 2 km. Competing destinations, intervening opportunities and tour models exist,
but either they were already tested here and did not help, or they are far from "simple on
purpose".

**4. Centre-only destinations?** Yes, it is real, and measurable with global data. In Paris, the 4
squares holding 10% of the jobs hold 24-40% of OSM's theatres, galleries, nightclubs, department
stores and clothes shops. They hold only 7-8% of its bakeries, supermarkets and pharmacies, which
follow where people live. London is the same, more steeply. US travel survey trips to a place four
or more times denser in jobs than home run 7-9 km at the median, against 1-2 km for trips within a
similar area. Your idea fits a gap T-637 left open. Real everyday trips are a mix of very short
trips and a long tail, which one distance curve cannot give. I did a scratch test on New York:
80% of outings stay local, and 20% go to "central" destinations (attraction weighted by its own
density, 6 km scale). Trips over 5 km go from 13% to 22% (survey 28%), trips under 2 km do not
change, and the share ending in the job core rises from 3.4% to 8.9%. Those are long trips into
dense places, which rail wins. It does not by itself cut short walking. It adds trips a train can
win.

## 1. What the fill-up is, purpose by purpose

| Purpose | Balancing | Distance curve | Fill-up? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commutes | doubly constrained (rows exact, every zone's jobs filled), pull capped at 20x since T-1000 | d^-a exp(-d/L) per pack, zone centres 2 km apart, own square at 1.04 km | yes |
| School | destination-constrained by enrolment | exp(-d/1.4 km) | yes (schools fill) |
| Errands, social, medical, university, airport | origin-constrained: each home shares its outings by attraction x exp(-d/scale) | W1 scales 1.3, 1.4, 2.0, 3.5, 20 km (T-637) | no |

So "destinations fill up" is true for commutes and school only. In a doubly constrained gravity a
job-rich centre gets a high pull, so it draws workers from far away. A balanced area's jobs get a
middling pull, and its residents mostly fill them, to the extent the curve keeps them near. The
curve does the keeping, and so far as the data shows it keeps too many: see 2.

## 4. What demand models usually do

- **Doubly constrained for work, singly for the rest.** The Wilson family (Wilson 1971, "A family
  of spatial interaction models", Environment and Planning 3): commutes doubly constrained (jobs
  and workers both known), shopping and leisure production-constrained with an attraction
  measure. NCHRP Report 716 (2012, *Travel Demand Forecasting: Parameters and Techniques*) gives
  US practice and defaults: gravity per purpose with gamma or exponential friction curves, home to
  work balanced to attractions. The game already does exactly this.
- **Destination choice with size terms and logsums** (Ben-Akiva and Lerman 1985; Daly 1982,
  "Estimating choice models containing attraction variables", Transportation Research B 16). A
  zone's pull is a size term (a weighted sum of job types, different per purpose) times the
  exponential of a utility that includes the mode-choice logsum. Activity-based models
  (CT-RAMP: Atlanta, SANDAG, Bay Area) keep work and school locations filled by **shadow prices**:
  an extra disutility on oversubscribed zones, iterated, which is doubly constrained in other
  words ([CCTA model wiki, ShadowPrice](https://github-wiki-see.page/m/contracostatransportationauthority/client_actc_ccta_model_doc/wiki/ShadowPrice)).
  Size terms differ by purpose and some models carry a separate mall term
  ([East-West Gateway destination choice model, 2014](https://www.ewgateway.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/DestinationChoiceModel_10_6_2014.pdf)).
  The logsum is what makes destinations respond to the network: T-454, parked by Anita.
- **Competing destinations** (Fotheringham 1983, Environment and Planning A 15): a destination
  near many others draws more (agglomeration, as in shopping districts) or less (competition).
  One fitted parameter; proposed here for the long-distance market (T-438), waiting on Anita.
- **Intervening opportunities** (Stouffer 1940; Schneider; the radiation model, Simini et al.
  2012, Nature 484): trips stop at the first suitable opportunity. Literally Anita's "fill up near
  home first". T-185 fitted its strength on LODES at 0 or below, so real people do *not* behave
  more like this than the gravity already has them.
- **Calibrating trip length.** The deterrence parameter is fitted to the observed trip length
  distribution, mean first (Hyman 1969, "The calibration of trip distribution models",
  Environment and Planning 1), then the whole distribution, intrazonal trips included. Where
  intrazonal trips come out wrong, practice adjusts the intrazonal impedance or adds K-factors
  (Ortuzar and Willumsen, *Modelling Transport*, ch. 5). Our curves outside the US were fitted on
  units much coarser than the zones, so that step is the missing one, and T-1091 is its simplest
  form.
- **Tours and activity chains** (T-187): a work-to-leisure stop on the way home, scheduling by
  time windows. Real, but a large model; far from "simple on purpose".

What fits "simple on purpose": the near-end change (a constant, no mechanism), per-purpose
attractions (already there), a two-part everyday draw (one share and one scale per purpose).
What does not: logsum destination choice (network-responsive, T-454), shadow-priced activity
models, tours, fitted competing-destination terms.

## 5. Destinations that exist only in centres

### Is it real?

- **Central place theory** (Christaller 1933; Berry and Garrison 1958, "The functional bases of
  the central place hierarchy", Economic Geography 34). A good's threshold (the market it needs)
  and range (how far people travel for it) rise with its order. High-order goods are offered in
  fewer, bigger centres, and people travel further for them. The empirical side: the
  "number-average size rule" (Mori, Nishikimi and Smith 2008, J. Regional Science), on Japan's
  industries: the fewer the cities an industry is in, the larger those cities are on average.
  The rule holds across industries and in the US (Hsu 2012). Within US metros, amenity counts
  scale non-linearly with population, differently by type (Kaufmann, Radaelli, Bettencourt and
  Shmueli 2022, "Scaling of urban amenities", [EPJ Data Science 11](https://epjdatascience.springeropen.com/articles/10.1140/epjds/s13688-022-00362-6);
  exponents by type not read here, the page would not load).
- **OSM, inside one city** (`osm_central.py`): the centre is the densest 2 km squares holding the
  first 10% of the pack's jobs (Paris: 4 squares, 18 km², 3.0% of residents; London: 2 squares,
  9 km², 0.6%). Share of each kind of OSM venue in them:

### How it could be done anywhere

1. **Anita's POI per 10k people.** Place one high-order venue per N residents, near them but drawn
   towards density (probability ~ density^k within the catchment). Its long-scale draw then pulls
   from far. Close to how a museum or department store gets sited, and it needs only population.
   Each venue is a random draw, though, so a city's centre moves between builds unless the draw
   is seeded, and N, k and the venue's draw are three new numbers.
2. **Each purpose split into local and central parts** (the scratch test). A share q of a
   purpose's outings uses a central attraction: the purpose's own attraction times its density
   (zone attraction / land), which piles it onto the densest places with no new data, at a longer
   scale. The rest stay on today's draw at a slightly shorter scale. Two numbers per purpose,
   deterministic, works wherever the purpose's attraction exists, and is the aggregate form of
   option 1.
3. **OSM high-order tags** (museum, theatre, arts centre, nightclub, department store, mall,
   clothes) as the central attraction. Truest to "you cannot do museum demand at the corner
   restaurant", but OSM completeness varies by country (the pipeline already scales by it for
   errands, T-453's c), and thinly mapped places would get no centre. Better as an ingredient of
   option 2's weight where OSM is complete than as the rule.

mean 2.95 against 2.91 km). "Central" = attraction x its density; "to centre" = trips ending in the

Social trips move the same way (80/20 at 6 km: 38.6 / 40.5 / 20.9%, mean 4.93, to centre 3.0% to
7.4%). The second scale makes the tail, and the density weight decides where it goes: without it
the long trips scatter (to centre 4.4% against 11.5%). Under 2 km stays where it is, because the
2 km squares floor the local part (T-688). So this answers "everything is interchangeable" and
gives rail long trips into dense places. It does not cut short walking.

**What it would do to rail.** The long trips end in the densest, paid-parking places, where rail
wins most easily; New York's everyday trips over 5 km would grow by about 60%. Anita's test A
rises (short trips are where a free fast train wins least; W1 cost A 2.3 points, which this
partly returns). On the real networks rail trips rise. The station counts are already about 3x New
York's and 2x London's counts, so that loose check gets worse.

**What it would cost.** Runtime: one more attraction per purpose and samples split between the
two parts (the sampler already draws by zone, then cell); about a day for code, tests and docs, no
pack rebuild if the central weight is computed when the everyday layer opens from arrays the packs
already carry. Option 3 adds a pipeline pass over OSM per row. A new complicating factor either
way, so it is Anita's call.
