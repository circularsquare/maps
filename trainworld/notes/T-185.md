# T-185: reverse commuting (diagnosis)

Anita (2026-10-12): her Camden point was city-centre residents commuting out to suburbs, not the
state line; she suspects the model has more reverse commuting than reality and doubts the T-162
border factor. Steers during the task: no absolute density classes as the yardstick (use relative
measures); intervening opportunities as the main candidate, as one strength on top of the
distance gravity, fitted to real data and undershooting rather than overshooting.

Diagnosis only, 2026-10-12. No pack, pipeline or sim change. The recommended curve shipped as
T-198 the same day (notes/T-198.md). All nine packs, LODES 8 OD 2023 (S000,
both ends in the pack's counties) as the yardstick, as in T-159/T-162.

## Answer in short

- **Reverse commuting is under-counted, not over-counted, in all nine packs.** Residents of
  job-rich places commute farther and more often outward than the shipped model says. Example:
  28% of Center City residents work outside Philadelphia (LODES), the model has 16%.
- **What Anita saw at Camden was the state line.** Before T-162, Center City residents were sent
  to NJ 1.7x too often but to the PA suburbs at half the real rate. The border factor removed the
  NJ excess (and overshot: NJ now 0.37x) and left the PA shortfall.
- **Intervening opportunities make the model worse in every pack.** Fitted to LODES, its strength
  comes out at zero (New York) or below zero (the other eight). Any positive strength moves every
  measure away from LODES. The strength to ship is 0: it closes none of the gap, because the gap
  runs the other way. Pure intervening-opportunity models (Schneider, radiation) are far worse
  than the gravity, and Schneider's one parameter does not carry between cities.
- **Keep the border factor.** No candidate touches the between-state share; without k, New York
  City residents working outside the city double (24% against LODES 13%).
- **The fix that does help is the shape of the distance curve**, not a new mechanism: a flatter
  near end, d^-0.8 exp(-d / 30 km) instead of d^-1 exp(-d / 40 km). It moves reverse commuting
  about 60% of the way to LODES (undershooting, as asked), and cuts trips misplaced by length by a
  third. A config value and a re-solve of the nine packs; about half an agent-day.

## 1. Yardsticks

All relative, so they work the same in New York and New Haven:

- **Local job ratio** of a home zone: jobs per resident commuter within 5 km. Under 0.5 is a
  bedroom area, over 2 a job centre. Bins are by ratio, not by absolute density.
- **Reverse ("downhill") commute**: a trip to a zone whose local job ratio is lower than home's.
- **Opportunity rank** of a trip: the number of jobs nearer to home than the work zone
  (Stouffer's intervening opportunities). The score "opportunity-rank misplaced" is the share of
  trips that would have to move between rank bins (own zone, under 1k, 1-3k, ... over 3M jobs) to
  match LODES, within each home ratio bin.
- T-162's two scores: trips misplaced by length (2 km bands) and between county pairs.
- As an illustration only, a centre / middle / suburb split: zones ranked by jobs, the densest
  holding the first 25% of the pack's jobs are "centre", the next 25% "middle", the rest
  "suburb" (`t185_classes.py`). Absolute density is not used anywhere.

commuter belt. It gives no reason to think the US figures overstate reverse commuting.

**LODES caveat.** LODES puts some multi-site employers' staff at the head office, usually in
the centre (T-034). That moves work ends inward, which would make LODES show *less* reverse
commuting than reality. So the under-count found here is, if anything, a lower bound.

## 3. The mechanism

1. **One decay curve, steep at short range.** d^-1 makes the own zone and its neighbours very
   attractive (a job 1 km away weighs four times one 4 km away before the exponential). For a
   home in a job centre, the nearest few zones hold a huge number of jobs, so the gravity keeps
   its residents there. Real job-centre residents take jobs about as far down the opportunity
   rank as anyone else: two-earner households, job matching by skill, and housing chosen for
   reasons other than the job all spread them out.
2. **Doubly constrained balancing reinforces it.** Centre jobs far outnumber centre residents,
   so their destination factors are large to pull in workers from far away. Centre residents
   get the same large factor on the jobs next door, so they stay even more (centre -> centre
   1.02-1.37x LODES). Since each class's jobs are fixed, fewer centre residents filling suburban
   jobs means suburbanites fill them, and suburban residents' trips come out too long.
3. **It is not the work-end constraint as such.** Relaxing it (origin-constrained, or half
   constrained) moves suburb -> centre to LODES but cuts reverse commuting further
   (0.73-0.78x) and leaves 4-8% of each zone's jobs unfilled or overfilled.

## 4. What the border factor fixes and what it costs

It fixes between-state flows, and nothing else in this study does. The between-state share of
commutes (LODES / k = 0.23 / k = 1): New York 8.5 / 8.3 / 23.9%, Philadelphia 10.1 / 10.4 /
27.1%, Providence 9.5 / 7.0 / 20.4%, Washington (k = 0.49) 24.9 / 25.0 / 35.6%. County pairs
misplaced: New York 7.5% with k against 16.9% without, Philadelphia 6.2% against 17.2%.

**Is its benefit really the centre/suburb effect? No.** Every intervening-opportunity strength
and every decay shape leaves the between-state share where k puts it: at k = 1 Providence stays
at 19.5-19.7% for strengths -0.2 to 0.2 (LODES 9.5%), Philadelphia 22.6-23.4%, New York 22.2-22.4%
with the flatter shapes. Inside one state, reverse commuting is under LODES with or without k.
So k and the reverse-commuting shortfall are separate errors. The Camden flows Anita saw were the
state-line one.

**What it costs** (each already known from T-159/T-162, now with the reason):

- Center City -> NJ falls to 2.2% against LODES 5.9% (0.37x; Center City -> Camden County 0.39x).
  One symmetric factor fits the big NJ -> PA flows and overshoots the small reverse ones.
- PATH (T-162: 48k against 212k a day) and Fairfield CT -> Manhattan (0.25x), Rockland -> Bergen
  (0.55x): cross-state flows a single factor makes too small.
- These are smaller than what k fixes. A future refinement could make the factor weaker for
  trips out of a job-rich zone, but nothing here measured that well enough to propose it.

Fitted strength: 0 in New York, at or below -0.2 everywhere else; positive strength is worse in
every pack and at +0.2 the length refit runs off to "no exponential" and still cannot give the
trip length back. Following Anita's rule (undershoot the measured strength), the strength to
ship is 0. The negative strength helps only because u grows with distance, so u^+0.2 flattens
the near end of the curve: a plain d^-0.6 or d^-0.8 does the same or better without any
opportunity count (rows above), so the gain belongs to the decay shape.

**Pure intervening-opportunity models do not carry between cities.** Schneider's best
acceptance rate per job is 1e-6 in New York, 3e-6 in Philadelphia, Boston, Washington and
Baltimore, and 1e-5 in Providence, New Haven, Hartford and Springfield: it scales with the size of
the job market, so one value does not fit nine cities. Even at each pack's own best it is worse
than the shipped gravity in every pack (sum of scores 19-58 against 16-35; close only in New Haven,
18.9 against 17.5, and Baltimore, 27.7 against 26.2), and it misplaces 8-25% of trips by
opportunity rank against 4-10%. The radiation model's trips are far too short (mean 2-8 km against 11-20). This agrees
with the literature: Masucci et al. (2013) found radiation worse than gravity at the scale of
commuting within England and Wales, and Lenormand, Bassolas and Ramasco (2016), comparing
gravity, intervening opportunities and radiation laws on commuting data from several countries,
found the gravity laws best and the constraint (doubly constrained) more important than the law.

## 6. Recommendation (Anita decides)

1. **Keep the border factor** (0.23, Washington 0.49). Nothing else fixes the between-state
   flows, and its costs (Center City -> NJ, PATH, Fairfield) are smaller than what it fixes.
2. **Do not add intervening opportunities.** LODES puts its strength at zero or below in all
   nine packs; any positive value makes the reverse-commuting shortfall worse.
3. **Optional: flatten the decay's near end to d^-0.8 exp(-d / 30 km), one value for all packs.**
   It closes about 60% of the reverse-commuting gap (centre -> suburb 0.83x to 0.93x of LODES,
   mean of packs), a third to half of the job-centre trip-length gap (New York 8.2 to 8.9 km of
   10.3, Philadelphia 9.8 to 10.8 of 11.8, Boston 7.1 to 7.9 of 9.1), and cuts trips misplaced by
   length from 7.0% to 4.6%. d^-0.7 exp(-d/25) would close about 75% (0.96x) at a slightly worse
   New York county score, which is why 0.8 is the cautious pick. New York is about neutral
   (sum of scores 16.2 now, 16.7; county pairs 7.5% to 8.7%); the other eight gain 2-9 points
   (Boston least, Providence most).
   Between-state shares stay near LODES at k = 0.23 (New York 8.5%, Philadelphia 10.8%), so k
   should be rechecked with T-162's bisection but is not expected to move much.

**Effort**: about half an agent-day. `build_city.py` (decay pow and length), `regions.py` re-solves
all nine packs in place in about 35 s (the pack format already carries `decay_pow`; no Rust
change), `read_pack.py` checks, rerun `t162_fit.py --final 0.8 30 0.23` for k, then T-162's
real-network comparison for New York (about 1.5 minutes a day) and the worker checks. Existing
saves' rider and fare numbers change on load.
