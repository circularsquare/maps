# T-2642: cheaper exact route searches for the market (design and measurement, 2026-11-06)

Agent searchcost. Stage 2 of T-2621 (notes/T-2621.md). Design, measurement and prototypes in a scratch copy of
the crate (main tree's sim at 23538b19); nothing in sim/ or app/ changed. "M" measured, "I" inferred, "G" a
guess.

Saves: all Japan (`../artifacts/trainworld/t2621/jp.net.json`: in the graph 9,253 stations, 20,640 route nodes,
29,893 nodes; 2.92M longer-trip pairs, 127M station pairs) and Anita's North America day 383
(`t2606/na383.net.json`: 1,613 stations, 4,270 route nodes). The market solved natively, period 0, one core,
at free flow and once more at a crowding (3 times the free-flow market loads, left behind on, so boarding
turns are exercised), against the main tree's registry, rows and packs.

**Timing caveat.** The machine was at 97% CPU all afternoon. The same base pass took 38 to 62 s of CPU in
different runs, so a run's sections are compared only with the run beside it, work is given as counts, and the
searches were timed by an interleaved benchmark (`t2642_search`: the modes take turns every 64 destinations,
order rotating, so the swings fall on all of them alike).

Files in `T-2642/`: `scratch.patch` (the scratch copy's changes: switches, counters and the prototypes in
kernel/search.rs and intercity/pass.rs), `t2642.rs` (the two tests, included at the end of the copy's
intercity/tests.rs), `run.cmd` and `launch.ps1` (how they were run, pinned to one core). Result lines:
`../artifacts/trainworld/t2642/*.txt` (jps = the search benchmark, jp1-jp3 and na2-na3 the passes).

## For Anita

**Short answer: yes, about 1.5 times on Japan's market pass, exactly.** Two changes do it.

1. **Stations served by one line with no walk (81% of Japan's stations, 77% of NA's) are worked out after the
   search, and the stops between them are stepped along the line without the queue.** Nothing reaches such a
   station in a search except its own line's trains, and nothing is reached from it, so it can wait to the end.
   Searches 1.47 times faster (1.51 with one more step, below), every strategy the same.
2. **The far-zone table a station click reads is filled one zone's column at a time while the pass runs**, then
   put back in its usual order. Today each longer trip adds into a row per start station, scattered over an
   87 MB table; by zone the adds land in one short column. Spreading the longer trips 3.8 times faster.

Together: Japan's pass about 1.42 times faster with every figure the same to the bit (except one rider-minutes
total, to 4e-14); with the extra step and two small fixes about 1.5 times, with a handful of loads off by one
float rounding (2e-7). Direct before/after runs on the busy machine gave 1.46-1.65 times. North America's
whole solve 1.35 times (its spreading 3 times faster too).

Nothing for you to decide. Two build stages are filed (T-2652, T-2653).

## 3. Why each is exact

**Leaf stations.** A station is a leaf when all its departures with trains are one line's, no walk comes into
it or leaves from its route nodes (no other station in walking distance, no complex), and every arrival there
is barred that whole line (`banned_all`, the departures barred non-empty), so its own `settle_station` relaxes
nothing. In the backward search such a station then only takes offers from its own route nodes and is read by
nobody. Its route nodes can only be reached by riding on (alighting there is barred), so each one's cost is the
next stop's plus the ride, set the moment the next stop settles. The destination itself is never a leaf in its
own search (its route nodes are reached by alighting).

- **Offers in queue order.** A leaf station's strategy is joined from its offers sorted by the key the queue
  would have popped them with (cost bits, then the id: the route node, or the boarding turn for a line with a
  left-behind or span wait), stopping at the first offer whose key comes after the station's own key, as the
  queue would have settled the station first. That is `join_line` in the same order on the same numbers, so
  the station's cost, accepted departures and next node are the same to the bit.
- **The settle order.** Stepped nodes and leaf stations sit at other places in `order`. The consumers need only
  every node after its next node (true: a run is stepped from its exit end, a leaf after its trains). Where
  they add (the assignment walk, the fare curve's extras), a stepped route node or leaf station's route node
  has at most two feeders (its station and the node riding into it), and two adds starting from 0 give the
  same float either way round. What does change is `Loads::pax_min`, one f64 total over every node in walk
  order: 1e-16 to 4e-13 relative.
- **The exit step** moves a run's first node next to the node it rides into; that node can have three or more
  feeders (its station and change nodes), so its flow's adds change order: one float rounding on a few loads.
  A run's last node stays queued in every mode, so the node it rides in from hears of it at the queue's time,
  and a tie between equal costs still goes the way it does today (a tie changing would move riders, not
  rounding).

**The table by zone.** Each entry gets the same adds in the same order (the start half lives only in the
by-zone copy during the pass, so the commutes' and outings' adds there interleave with the longer trips' as
before), and the copy back to row order puts each sum into an entry that is still 0. Same to the bit,
measured.

## 4. What others do (looked at outside)

No source found that steps a hyperpath (optimal strategies) search along single-line runs without its queue;
the pieces are known separately.

- **RAPTOR** (Delling, Pajor, Werneck 2012, https://i11www.iti.kit.edu/extra/publications/dpw-rbptr-12.pdf):
  rounds by number of transfers, each route scanned stop by stop with no priority queue. Timetable based, not
  pooled frequencies, so not usable as it is; the shared idea is that along a route labels follow line order.
- **Connection Scan** (Dibbelt, Pajor, Strasser, Wagner 2013/2018, https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.05997): one pass over
  connections sorted by time. Needs a timetable; nothing to sort by in a frequency model.
- **Spiess and Florian 1989** (https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/transb/v23y1989i2p83-102.html): the search is
  label-setting from a priority structure, the loading runs the accepted links in reverse; a heap-free run is
  an implementation detail, not a change of algorithm. A Rust port (hyperpaths-rs,
  https://docs.rs/crate/hyperpaths-rs/latest) notes that a strict or non-strict update test changes which links
  count as attractive on ties: why the exit step keeps a run's last node queued.
- **EMME and Visum** document the strategy and headway-based models (transit segments at nodes, a wait per
  boarding) but nothing found on how their searches avoid queue work (EMME's manual could not be fetched).
- **Degree-2 chain contraction** in road routers: OSRM compresses chains of degree-2 nodes before routing
  (graph_compressor.cpp); customizable contraction hierarchies note that road graphs are full of them
  (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.0402). The leaf stations here are the transit form, done on the fly because a
  run's costs depend on its exit's.
- **Faster hyperpaths** (Bell's Hyperstar 2009, https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:transb:v:43:y:2009:i:1:p:97-107;
  Ma, Fukuda and Schmöcker 2013) are goal-directed for one origin, no use for one-to-all; the hyperbush
  algorithm (Xu et al. 2022, https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ortrsc:v:56:y:2022:i:4:p:877-903) is for
  equilibrium assignment. Dial or radix queues need integer or monotone keys (T-1647 left the radix queue for
  rounding). Nothing found on exact reuse between destinations.

The literature summary is a helper's, mostly from abstracts and search summaries; the links were checked to
exist, not every claim.

## 5. Recommendation and stages

Build both; the order does not matter (they touch different files) and either alone is worth it: the leaf
stations speed every search in the game (the cities' and outings' too, not only the market's), the table only
the market. Not recommended: the spreading turned round, bounded searches.

1. **T-2652, leaf stations and single-line runs in the strategy search** (kernel/search.rs; Rust, Sonnet with
   this note and the patch). Leaf stations found once per graph in `Search::new`; route nodes at them stepped
   from a run's first node (queued) to its last (queued); leaf stations' strategies after the queue, offers
   joined in queue-key order, put last in the settle order; the destination never a leaf; then the exit step.
   A sim switch to turn it off for A/B. **Done when**: in an interleaved benchmark (as `t2642_search`) Japan's
   searches at least 1.4 times faster (pops 271M to at most 125M); on Japan, North America and the kernel tests'
   networks every strategy the same (route costs, planned-wait entries, accepted departures, next nodes, change
   nodes; the settle order the same nodes) without the exit step, and with it every load within one float
   rounding (2e-7) and rail by pair to the bit; cargo tests pass.
2. **T-2653, the far-zone table's start half filled by zone** (intercity/pass.rs, the pattern in api.rs; Rust,
   Sonnet). The by-zone index (each zone's column of start stations) built with the pattern, once per network;
   the zone's own column found through a station map, the commutes' and outings' trips home looked up once a
   pair; the copy back to row order at the end of the pass (or the click reader taught the layout, if the
   copy's memory matters: about 45-65 MB more on Japan's market worker during a pass, I: the start half, about
   half the table's 21.7M entries, held twice, plus its station ids). **Done when**: Japan's spreading at least 3 times faster, commutes no slower, and the table
   and every other figure the same to the bit; with T-2652, **Japan's native pass at least 1.5 times faster**
   (adjacent before/after runs, median of three rounds) at the same figures to rounding.

What is left after both, should more be wanted (G): `settle_station` on the other 19% of stations (now the
largest single step), the planned-wait entry costs (7% of a search), the logsums' exp per station pair, the
assignment walks.
