## Why trains looked like they never fill

1. **A full train looked like any other.** The gauge fills dark from the back: half means seats
   taken, full means crush. A train at 85% of crush is 90% dark; at crush, 100% dark, which is
   also exactly how a train without load data is drawn. The red over-full edge could only appear
   on demand's fallback fill, because the account never loads a train past crush (whoever does not
   fit waits). So full trains had no look of their own.
2. **On the real network few trains reach crush**: 3% of morning-peak trains, 8% in the evening
   peak. The capacities are fine (8-10 car subway trains, 13-car commuter trains, crush 160 a
   car, about the MTA's own loading guideline per 20 m), and the account's in-period shape is
   sharper than demand's (arrivals peak at 1.8 times the period's mean at 08:00 and 18:00, against
   the 1.4 that demand judges crowding at; demand's busiest-hour factors do reach the account:
   1.4, 1.15, 1.4, 1.3, 1.5). What keeps trains below crush is demand: its crowding penalty
   (docs/demand.md step 7, alpha 2) starts once seats run out, so riders move to other routes or
   the car well before crush, and the equilibrium sits between seated and crush loads; and demand
   carries fewer subway riders than the real city (2.74M a day after T-198, commutes and everyday
   trips; T-090 had the busiest subway links at 76% of their measured busiest hour).
3. **A busy new line does fill**: the Manhattan trunk is packed on every peak train and leaves
   10,000-25,000 riders behind in the peak hour. So in a new game crowding shows where a line is
   short of trains; on the real network it shows at about 30 platforms.
4. **Platform crowds draw from zoom 14** (T-199), red only while the last train left someone
   behind.
