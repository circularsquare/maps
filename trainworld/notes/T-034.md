# T-034 fit the access constants and the rail constant: paused, the gravity comes first

Started and paused 2026-10-09 by the demand realism agent. **No constant was changed.** Before
fitting the rail constant and the other access leg against the real-network comparison
(notes/T-007.md), I checked what each lever does and what the over-prediction is made of. The
answer is that a large part of it is the zone gravity sending New Jersey and Staten Island
residents across the water to Manhattan at about twice the real rate, which no access constant
should be bent to absorb. Fitting on top of it would be stacking a fit on a broken base, so this
waits for T-072. Everything below is measured and reusable once T-072 lands.

Tools added: `realnet --set name=value,...` (any of the mode-choice constants; `Params::set_extra`),
`pipeline/realnet_groups.py` (rail share by county group against ACS for several runs),
`pipeline/road_speed.py` (ACS car commute minutes by county against the gravity's distances, and a
fit of road speed by density), `pipeline/county_flows.py` (county-to-county flows: model, LODES OD,
ACS 2016-2020).

The model's numbers are commutes only, the operators' all trips (commuter rail is mostly commutes).

- **The rail constant is not the problem**: the four big boroughs are right at 1.5 and fall to
  25% (ACS 42%) at 0, while the suburbs stay over.
- **The other access leg is most of the suburban excess.** Walk-only access lands Long Island,
  Westchester and Connecticut and the outer counties on ACS, and commuter rail near its measured
  riders. That is partly luck (real commuter rail is half park-and-ride, so the walk catchment
  must be doing too much), but it says the other leg as priced adds rail where none is measured:
  it is as easy to drive to a Manhattan station as to a Long Island car park.
- **NJ inner stays 60% over even with walking only** (13.5% against 8.5%). That is the gravity
  (section 3).
- **Manhattan is 13-14 points over whatever the access**: its residents walk to work in ACS (24%)
  and the model's walk curve gives them a few percent once rail exists (T-058's walk curve).

| Suffolk | 91% | 29.1 | 25.7 | 52.9 |
| Ocean | 95% | 30.4 | 30.1 | 59.4 |

Car commutes take 27-37 minutes everywhere, while the gravity's commutes get longer the further
out one lives. The model drives at 28 km/h x 1.3 detour everywhere (21.5 km/h crow-fly, plus 3
minutes to park), so a Suffolk commute takes it 75 minutes against ACS's 29. A road speed falling
with density fits the counties within 5.5% rms, but only with an implausible 170 km/h between the
dense ends of a trip (`road_speed.py --fit`): the gravity's suburban commutes are too long for any
real speed. That is the same gravity problem again (section 3), and the road speed should be fitted
after it is fixed.

Two things are wrong, one under the other:

1. **The crow-fly gravity ignores the water.** Hudson, Bergen, Essex, Passaic and Union send
   1.9-4.3x as many commuters to Manhattan as ACS (and LODES) say, because they are close to
   Manhattan in km and the gravity cannot see the Hudson with its handful of tunnels and bridges;
   Westchester, on the same side as Manhattan, sends too few. Those trips are what makes NJ inner
   rail 60% over even with walking only, and a good part of the single Manhattan line's
   park-and-ride riders.
2. **LODES OD itself puts too few people in their own county**: 42.8% against ACS's 51.6% (less
   work from home). LODES places a job at the establishment an employer reports, so multi-site
   employers (governments, chains, staffing and home-care agencies, T-014, T-043) put local workers
   at a distant head office; ACS asks the worker. T-006 fitted the decay to LODES's lengths, so the
   gravity inherits commutes that are too long; to Manhattan, the one place nobody misreports, all
   three agree on the total (24-28%).

So the decay's target and its distance measure both need attention before the access and road
constants mean anything. Proposed as **T-072** (TASKS.md): county-pair factors (K-factors, a
standard gravity fix) fitted so the model's county-to-county flows match ACS less work from home,
shipped in the pack as a region id per zone and a small factor table (one extra lookup per zone
pair in the pair loop), and the decay refitted on lengths within those pairs. A barrier term from
the water mask is the alternative; K-factors are simpler and fit the data directly.

## What T-034 does after T-072, in order

1. Refit the road speed by density (`road_speed.py --fit`) on the corrected distances.
2. Price the other access leg with parking at the station's end (the same job-density parking cost
   the road mode pays at a destination), so it works at suburban car parks and not in Midtown.
3. Consider a bus without parking inside "other" (SPEC 4.3 counts bus there; today "other" is
   priced as a car parked at the destination, which overstates rail into Manhattan from places
   with good buses: ACS bus shares Hudson 17%, Bronx 18%, Staten Island 20%).
4. Then fit the rail constant and the other leg's constants against the county shares and the
   commuter-rail totals; access-mode shares (park-and-ride surveys) as the check.

Anita's SPEC 12 question (how forgiving drive and bus access to stations should be): the data
says less forgiving than now. As priced, the other leg carries 25% of rail trips and puts the
suburbs at 2x their measured rail share; walking only puts them on ACS. Not changed yet.
