# T-093 — a bucket queue in the search, every level

2026-11-07. The rule is in docs/canoe.md 5 and the comment on `Buckets` in `js/search.js`; this
note has the design reasoning and the measurements.

## What changed

- `js/search.js`: both loops (`solveWalk`, `solve`) pop from `Buckets`, Dial's bucket queue, in
  place of the binary heap (walking loop) and the 4-ary heap (general loop). `edgeFloor` gives the
  bucket width; `queueInfo` holds the last solve's width, buckets, re-queues and entries, for
  checks. The queue's arrays (bucket heads, pos, the entry pool) are kept for the next solve.
- `js/levels.js`: `setDirs` records `L.sdMin` (the fastest directed hours per km), `setSlope`
  `L.slopeMin` (the least slope factor, min Tobler factor x least height factor squared).
- `js/canoe.js`: `buildCanoe` records `cn.curMax` (strongest current) and `cn.rDtMin` (quickest
  river edge).

## Design

Weights are time + K x hazard with K = 1000 h, so edges span 0 to hundreds of hours, and
`wStop` = maxHours + K x hzStop = 5,629 h with hazard on.

- **Dial's buckets, width d under the lightest edge.** If every edge weighs at least d, a node
  popped from bucket b can't be improved by another node of b, so each node is expanded once at
  its final weight, exactly as by a heap; order within a bucket (last in, first out) only breaks
  ties differently. d comes from a lower bound per solve, not from a guess: the shortest move in
  the level times the fastest hours per km any land cell has (`L.sdMin`, else the fastest speed
  byte), times the least slope factor, times carry; on a canoe start also the transfer (0.25 h)
  and paddling at paddle + the strongest current both ends could add. Less a float32 margin
  (wStop x 2^-23, then x (1 - 1e-6)), since weights are stored as float32. Hazard only adds.
- **River edges.** The L10 canoe layer has river edges of 0 km (rDt 0; 14 under 0.01 h of 2.0 M),
  so a floor over them would be 0. They are left out of d when they would make it too small, and
  the river loop alone also relaxes nodes already popped from the current bucket and re-queues one
  it improves (`again`). Weights stay least whatever d; the results are the heap's exactly while
  again is 0, which it was in every solve measured.
- **Fallback.** If d would need over 2^21 buckets, or a level lacks its minima (NaN), d =
  wStop / 2^21 and every edge relaxes popped nodes of the current bucket (label-correcting within
  the bucket). Weights are still least; with hazard on, a node's stop rule (time over maxHours or
  hazard over hzStop) may have read an earlier, heavier label, so times near the stop rule could
  differ. Checked by forcing it (a scratch copy with exact off, and one with 2^12 buckets: 6.2 M
  re-queues on the L1 window): weights equal to the heap's in every node, times within 4.5e-5.
- **Lazy entries.** A node whose weight falls into another bucket gets a new entry in a pool and
  its old one is skipped on pop (pos no longer names that bucket). It was a little faster than
  doubly linked lists with unlinking (walking L0.1 1.34x against 1.16x in one run).
- **Why not a radix heap**: also exact, but every pop redistributes a bucket; Dial with a proven
  width is exact at O(1) a pop, and the floor proof is cheap (minima recorded when a level is
  built).
- **Reused arrays.** In Chrome the first version (allocating the pool fresh each solve, growing
  1 M -> 4 M entries) was slower than the heap at L0.1 in all four session pairs (3-36%), though
  faster in node: the worker's heap is large (tile caches, L10's 120 MB of directions), and the
  extra allocation costs GC there. Keeping the arrays (at most two spare sets) fixed it. Part of
  the Chrome gain below may be the reuse itself, which the heap never had.

## Times

The machine was over-full the whole time (the core ledger at 16-19 of 16), so absolute times are
inflated and noisy; runs interleave the two searches.

**Node** (CPU time of the process, median of 6, heap -> bucket; ms):

| Case | Heap | Bucket | x |
|---|---|---|---|
| A L10 walk, hazard on | 642 | 547 | 1.17 |
| B L10 walk, hazard off | 531 | 407 | 1.30 |
| C L10 canoe | 1764 | 1797 | 0.98 |
| D L1 2 M cells | 2906 | 2359 | 1.23 |
| E L0.1 1.51 M, hazard on | 2187 | 1907 | 1.15 |
| F L0.1 1.51 M, hazard off | 2063 | 1578 | 1.31 |
| G L1 canoe 1.21 M | 2297 | 1938 | 1.19 |

Other runs gave 1.0-1.5x for the same cases (a 12-rep run: C 1.13, D 1.25, E 1.38). A CPU
profile of D and E: the heap's code was 18-20% of its solve's time, the bucket queue's 11-12%.
The rest is the edge costs, so a queue alone could never give much more than ~1.25x on the
general loop; T-030's 1.3-1.6x was on a plain walking loop with a binary heap.

**Headless Chrome** (`notes/T-093/chrome.mjs`, 1400 x 900, four sessions each, alternating; a
session: walking start at Cluj, zoom 8, six L0.1 windows at the cap round Cluj at zoom 9.5 as in
T-039, then a canoe start at Constanta and zoom 8), solve ms:

| | Heap | Bucket |
|---|---|---|
| L0.1 at the cap (1.51 M), 24 windows | 1430-2509, median 1960 | 1131-2203, median 1416 (1.38x) |
| L0.1, first window of a session | 1430, 1899, 1931, 2403 | 1199, 1286, 1402, 1748 |
| Walking L10 click | 524, 595, 664, 756 | 415, 443, 502, 507 |
| L1 at zoom 8 (0.27 M) | 284, 307, 313, 417 | 209, 216, 238, 248 |
| Canoe L10 click | 1397, 1616, 1868, 2134 | 1080, 1259, 1912, 2062 |
| Canoe L1 at zoom 8 (0.27 M) | 306, 331, 334, 353 | 230, 241, 257, 294 |

T-039 measured L0.1 at the cap at 1.47-2.05 s (median 1.64) on a less loaded machine; with the
bucket queue it is 1.42 s median here under heavier load. The canoe L10 click gains least
(~1.1x in Chrome, none in node): its cost is the sea edges (T-035).
