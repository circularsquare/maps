# T-071 working notes: upstream paddling and carrying speeds

Anita (2026-11-05): "it's kinda weird to me that paddling up is slower than walking/carrying. is
it really". The model was upstream = max(5 - 0.6 v, 1.5) km/h, so 2.0 km/h on a huge river, under
carrying on grass (4.86 / 2 = 2.4). Now max(5 - 0.4 v, 2.5) km/h; carry stays x2.

## The answer

Partly yes. Going up a big fast river was slow: recorded crews made about 25-45 km a day in
10-16 hour days, so 2.5-3.5 km/h per moving hour, below walking on a trail (4-5 km/h) and about
level with carrying a canoe on one (single carry ~3 km/h). People still went up by river because
there was rarely a trail, the loads were heavy, and the river was the trail. But the old model
was too slow on big rivers: it charged 0.6 of the section-mean current, and the mean is what the
middle of the channel does. Along the bank of a big river, in a metre or two of water and in the
eddies, the current is a quarter to two fifths of the mean. So 2.0 km/h on a huge river was low;
3.0 is nearer the record.

Where the search still carries beside a big river, it is mostly meanders and roads: the river is
1.3-1.6 x longer than a straight line, and carrying on today's roads or open land runs at
2.4-2.5 km/h on that straighter line. In 10,000 BC (no roads, T-010) carrying gets slower.

Not found within the search budget: an Amazon or Orinoco small-canoe upstream log with daily
distances (Wallace, Bates and Spruce mostly sailed upriver on the trade wind or give no rates),
Yukon poling logs, Congo or Mekong figures. Steve Posselt kayaked up the Mississippi from the
Gulf (3 Mar 2015) to Memphis (11 Apr) in high water, ~1,200 km in 39 days, ~31 km a day, from a
one-line magazine summary (bigrivermagazine.com, now 404): consistent with the 2015 canoes.

**Up/down ratios** where both directions are recorded: the Hayes 1.7, the Saskatchewan-Churchill
route 1.75 (both with lakes and portages, which cost the same either way), the Mackenzie stem
alone ~3-4. The model's river-only ratio on a Q = 700 river is (5 + 3.8) / 3.5 = 2.5.

**Bank current.** With u ~ h^(2/3) across the section and D = 0.27 Q^0.39 (docs/canoe.md 2): in
1.5 m of water the margin current is ~1.0 x the mean at Q = 100 (D 1.6 m, no shallow margin),
~0.4 at Q = 2,200 (D 5.6 m), ~0.25 at Q = 17,000 (D 12 m). Crossings at bends to the inside bank
meet ~1.2 x the mean over maybe 15-20% of the length. Overall 0.3-0.5 on big rivers; small
rivers have no slack margin but also a slow current. Shiono and Knight 1991 (J. Fluid Mech. 222)
is the lateral-shear model; this is the textbook reading of it, not a measurement.

## Decision

upShare 0.6 -> **0.4**, upMin 1.5 -> **2.5** km/h, carry **2** kept. Upstream now: 4.3 km/h at
Q = 1, 3.8 at 100, 3.3 at 2,200, 3.0 at 10,000, 2.9 at 17,000, 2.5 (floor) from ~68,000 (the
Amazon). The old table: 3.9 / 3.2 / 2.4 / 2.0 / 1.8 / 1.5. Tried also 0.5 / 2.5 (huge rivers
2.5, a little under the crews' 2.7-3.1; the Missouri trip still carried 62%).

Thin spots: every recorded trip is a crew, most in heavier boats than the model's canoe; moving
hours are guessed from day lengths; no small-canoe upstream log on a really huge river (Amazon,
Congo) was found. Small rivers may be too fast upstream (4.3 km/h at Q = 1): Lewis and Clark
dragged the dugouts up the shallow Jefferson. That is the small-river question as a whole
(downstream too), not this task.
