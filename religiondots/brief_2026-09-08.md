## 1. The four open asks

### 001 — Egypt. Copts at governorate level, or not at all.

**The question.** Egypt is buildable today from pooled Arab Barometer waves III, IV, V and VII:
6,840 respondents, religion answered by 100% of them, cut by governorate, public files with no
account. It clears every bar this project applies to a survey-built country. The scout stopped
short of drawing it because §14.4 rule 2 says no resolution finer than the state's own
publication, and Copts are a persecuted minority.

**What is genuinely new.** Three of the four grounds on which §11d closed Egypt have moved. The
important one cuts *against* drawing: CAPMAS's own deposited 2017 census file has 13 variables
and religion is not among them, so there is no state microdata route at any geography. It is
the Arab Barometer or nothing.

**The evidence that it would be a good map.** The instrument is not undercounting Copts, which
was the thing to fear. Weighted national Christian share 5.93% against the 1986 census's
5.7–5.8%; Cairo governorate 8.50% against the census's 8.57% in 1996. Split-half rank
correlation +0.495 against a +0.418 bar. The ordering is the real Coptic geography: Sohag 16.9%,
Minya 16.1%, Asyut 14.8%.

**Three ways to rule.** Draw at governorate (best map, clearest rule 2 problem); draw Egypt as
one national unit at 5.9% Christian with the Upper Egypt concentration stated in prose and not
drawn, which is how Türkiye's Alevi figure is handled; or leave it closed with the route on
record.

**Also worth knowing:** this now blocks four more countries. The leads triage queued Lebanon,
Iraq, Jordan and Yemen behind it as the same question, and Lebanon has had no census since 1932.

**The filing agent's own read:** option 2, on the grounds that it follows existing precedent
most closely. It said it had no view it would defend against hers.

### 002 — South Africa. A free DataFirst account.

**The question.** South Africa is drawn at nine provinces, 6.1M people per unit, the coarsest
counting geography on the map. DataFirst catalogue 611 holds the same survey's person file with
the same 24 categories at local-municipality level: 26 times the spatial detail, same year, same
instrument. It needs a registered account and a signed confidentiality declaration in her name.

**Why nine provinces really is the open ceiling.** Every alternative was opened and read rather
than inferred. Report 03-01-84 is province-only and coarser; Census 2011 asked no religion
question at all, which kills every 2011 municipal product; and Stats SA's keyless Census 2022 API
serves 24 topics down to Main Place with religion on none of them. In the Census 2022 provincial
profiles, religion is the single variable published province-only while everything else goes to
district and municipality. That reads as a decision rather than a gap.

**What it buys.** At nine units the map can say Limpopo is 50.8% African Independent Church
against Western Cape's 15.8%. It cannot say anything about Soweto against Sandton, or the old
Transkei against the Eastern Cape coast, which on this country is most of what a reader wants.

**One trap if she signs up:** take file 611, not 982. Census 2022's 10% sample is behind the same
account and more recent, but leaves Christianity undivided, so it trades the whole denominational
split for six years of currency.

**This is the only one of the four that cannot be done for her.**

### 003 — China. Tibet is one census behind.

**The question.** Not a defect. The Tibet audit concluded the map is correct as drawn. This
reports that one of the two reasons behind the 5 September decision to use 2010 has stopped
being true for this one region, and asks whether that is worth acting on.

**The gap.** The map draws the Tibet Autonomous Region at 8.17% Han, which is 2010 exactly. 2020
says 12.15%. That is about 198 further grey dots.

**Why the reasoning is weaker here.** The 2010 choice rested partly on 2010 to 2020 being close
to a uniform rescale, so the vintage moves magnitude and not geography. Across China that holds.
In Tibet it does not: Han grew 80.8% over the decade and Tibetan 15.5%, so the vintage moves the
one ratio a reader of this region is most likely looking at. Xinjiang is presumably the same
case and was not checked.

**And the input is easier than assumed.** The blocker was that the national province by
nationality table exists only as a 3 MB JPEG scan. Still true of that table, but each of Tibet's
seven prefectures publishes the nationality breakdown in HTML in its own 2020 communiqué. No OCR
needed.

**What it costs.** A re-scatter and retile of China, 1.26M dots, about an hour mostly waiting.
The real cost is a mixed vintage inside one country, which is the specific thing the original
decision was avoiding, and it would be the third reconciliation rule in China after Hainan.

**The filing agent's own read:** leave it. The region is 86% Tibetan on either vintage and the
error is four points on a category that draws grey.

### 004 — Armenia. Does the Armenian church get its own node?

**The question as filed.** Armenia puts 2,793,041 people on the bare `christianity.oriental`
parent, which makes the node about 95% Armenian. The argument for the parent was that no source
separates the Oriental Orthodox churches finely enough to justify children.

**What the review found, which changes the shape of the question.** The branch already has ten
children, all with dots on them, and one of them is already the Armenian Apostolic Church.
`.ethiopian` holds 35,419 dots against the bare parent's 3,351, so the map already files a home
country's national church below the parent, and it is the biggest body on the branch.

**And the live inconsistency.** The Armenian Apostolic Church is drawn two ways on the shipped
map today: at the bare parent for Armenia, Georgia, Canada, Australia, Bulgaria, Cyprus, Czechia,
Poland and Romania, and at `.armenian-etchmiadzin` for the United States, France, Spain, Greece
and Finland. That is true right now, independent of how she rules.

**The caveat that cuts the other way.** Both existing Armenian nodes are labelled for American
jurisdictions, sourced only to the US religion census. They are ASARB's US dioceses rather than
the worldwide church, so putting Armenians in Lyon on a North American diocese is already a
stretch and Armenia's 2.79 million would be a bigger one. A clean fix therefore needs a general
`.armenian` node with the two US jurisdictions merged under it, which is a bigger edit than the
six mapping files the ask prices, and is a real reason to prefer leaving it alone if she wants
this small.

**Note it goes against her `todo.txt` line about getting rid of some Jewish categories** — this
proposes adding a legend row, not removing one, though the review's finding is that the row is
already there.

## 3. The stability test — no, it should not block anything

She is right, and the record agrees with her more than my summary implied.

**What happened.** Finland is drawn from pooled European Social Survey rounds. Applying this
project's own stability test for the first time to an ESS country, `christianity.lutheran`
scores +0.44 against a +0.462 bar. Unaffiliated and Orthodox both pass.

**Why it is not a crisis.** The test's own treatment for this case, in §14.16, is to *name the
weakness in the note* — not to withhold the country, not to redraw it. Chi-square says the
Finnish regions differ overwhelmingly regardless. The pattern being drawn is real; what the test
is saying is that the ordering of the middle regions on one category is not stable between
halves of the sample, which is a fair thing to admit and not a reason to unship a country.

**So the only action Finland needs, if any, is one clause in `note_public`** saying the
between-region ordering on Lutheran affiliation is not firm. Its note already names sample
thinness. This is optional.

**The one thing actually worth doing is not about Finland.** Greece, Italy, France and Spain are
also drawn from ESS and **none of them has ever had the stability test run at all**. That is the
gap. Running it on those four is cheap and read-only, files no ask, and answers the question she
actually asked: whether anything is *crazy* off. If all four pass, the matter closes and the
note stays as it is. If one is far below the bar, that is a country worth looking at on its own
merits.

**Recommendation: run the test on `gr`, `it`, `fr` and `es`, do nothing else, and treat a pass
as closing the subject.**

## 4. Re-sweeping the closed rows — agreed, and not yet

Her call to work through more of the queue first is recorded here so the next session does not
re-propose it.

For when it does happen: six sweep negatives were overturned in one day, and the mechanism is
worth stating because it is not luck. A closure records the tier or the publication series it
tested, not the country — and then it hides the country from later sweeps. Finland is the clean
case. A later sweep went hunting for exactly the route that would have found it, listed eight
Nordic countries, and omitted Finland *because* the earlier sweep had already crossed it off.
The one pass that would have caught the error was the pass that had been told not to look.

The six: Eswatini (volumes on the finance ministry's upload folder), Botswana (2011 per-district
booklets outside the census report set), South Africa (a bot wall read as a dead host), Moldova
(the row priced the wrong census), Armenia (the office's English site is a strict subset of its
Armenian one), Finland (the register closure was correct and the survey tier was never tested).
